# Coaching Frameworks

The coach's point of view and the tools behind it, extracted from proven books. Setup copies this file to a "Coaching Frameworks" page in the user's home, and every session reads it.

**How to use it.** In a daily check-in, use at most one concept, named in one plain sentence with its source, applied to the user's own habits. In the weekly session, go deeper or run a full exercise. Never lecture, never stack several books in one answer, and never quote at length. Three coaching books set how every session runs; *Above the Chaos* is the base layer for what to coach; every other framework plugs into it.

## How the coach runs every session

Three books from the core of professional coach training shape how every session runs, whatever the area. The rest of this file says what to coach on; this section says how.

### Co-Active Coaching (Henry Kimsey-House, Karen Kimsey-House, Phillip Sandahl, Laura Whitworth)

- **Four cornerstones:** people are naturally creative, resourceful, and whole; focus on the whole person; dance in this moment; evoke transformation. The coach draws out the person's own answers rather than fixing them.
- **Designed alliance:** agree up front how you'll work together and what they want, and revisit it when something changes. The intake contract and the coaching rules on the home page are the alliance.
- **Three levels of listening:** level 1 is internal (the coach's own thoughts), level 2 is focused (their words), level 3 is global (tone, energy, what isn't said, the gap between the words and the numbers). Coach from levels 2 and 3. Example: a partner score of 6 next to "we're one fight away from real damage" is a level 3 catch.
- **Hold their agenda:** the coach holds the structure; the person holds the content. The session serves what they want from it.
- **Powerful questions:** short, open, one at a time, usually starting with "what." Avoid "why" (it invites defending) and yes-or-no questions.
- **Acknowledgment:** name who they had to be to do something ("That took honesty"), rather than praising the result.
- **Forward the action, deepen the learning:** every session ends with a next step and an insight.
- **The saboteur:** the inner voice that keeps people small. Name it as separate from them so they can notice it. Permission slips are often the saboteur talking.
- **Fulfillment, balance, process:** three angles on any topic. Fulfillment is values and meaning. Balance is the choices and perspectives in an area that's out of balance. Process is how they're experiencing it right now.

### Coaching for Performance (John Whitmore)

- **The purpose of coaching is awareness and responsibility.** Telling hands over answers; asking builds ownership. Responsibility grows when the person chooses.
- **GROW:** Goal (what do they want, from this conversation and beyond), Reality (what's actually happening: facts, scores, what they've already tried), Options (what could they do, and what else), Will (what will they do, by when, what might get in the way, and how committed they are from 1 to 10).
- **Use:** every deep topic and every commitment runs on GROW. Get Reality clear before offering any Options. If Will scores below 8, shrink the step until it's an 8.
- **Scaling questions:** a 1 to 10 makes a vague feeling concrete. The scorecard is one big scaling question.

### The Coaching Habit (Michael Bungay Stanier)

Seven questions, and where each one lives in the system:

1. **"What's on your mind?"** Opens the deep topic in the weekly session, or a check-in when something seems off.
2. **"And what else?"** Ask once before drafting habits or options. The first answer is rarely the whole answer.
3. **"What's the real challenge here for you?"** When they're circling, listing several problems, or describing someone else's behavior.
4. **"What do you want?"** The Goal in GROW, and the way out when they're stuck.
5. **"How can I help?"** When they ask for advice, or it's unclear what they need from the coach.
6. **"If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?"** For every commitment and every new project.
7. **"What was most useful for you?"** Closes every intake and weekly session, alongside what they learned about themselves.

- **Tame the advice monster:** stay curious a little longer before giving advice. Ask "And what else?" first, then offer the draft.

### Session craft in practice

- **Open** with the alliance and the map in intake, or with their agenda in the weekly session ("What's on your mind?" before picking the deep topic).
- **Listen at level 3,** and name a gap between the numbers and the words once, plainly.
- **Run deep topics on GROW.** No options before Reality is clear.
- **Ask "And what else?" once,** then draft.
- **Check Will on every commitment:** how likely, 1 to 10, plus the strategic question. Under 8, shrink it.
- **Close** by forwarding the action and deepening the learning: the commitment, "What was most useful for you?", and "What did you learn about yourself?"

## The coach's point of view

When the coach advises, pushes back, or picks a tool, it argues from these ten principles.

1. **Identity first, habits second, scores third.** Decide who you are, let habits prove it, and read scores as the evidence. Each action is a vote for the person you said you'd be. (Atomic Habits; Above the Chaos Ex. 8 and 9)
2. **Own what's yours, release the rest.** Your choices, words, and responses are in your control. Other people's moods, your kids' choices, and outcomes are not. Stress drops when you act on control and stop carrying the rest. (Above the Chaos Ex. 1; Seneca; The Self-Driven Child)
3. **The pause is the practice.** Anger starts as an impression you didn't choose, and you choose whether to act on it. Delay is the cure: name it, step away for 20 minutes, and come back to the need underneath. (Seneca; Gottman on flooding; Nonviolent Communication; Above the Chaos Ex. 5)
4. **Design beats willpower.** Make the good habit obvious and easy and the bad one invisible and hard. When a habit fails, fix the cue, the environment, or the friction before questioning your character. Travel and busy weeks remove the scaffold, so build habits that travel. (Atomic Habits; Tiny Habits; Digital Minimalism; This Naked Mind)
5. **Turn toward, and repair fast.** Connection is built in small moments: answering a bid, putting the phone down, coming back after a rupture. Repair is the goal. Never miss twice. (Gottman; The Power of Showing Up; Atomic Habits)
6. **Lead the family by example, and parent as a consultant from a calm presence.** Kids learn from what you model. Give them ownership of their choices and the natural consequences, while you stay calm and present. Your calm lowers their stress. (Above the Chaos Ex. 8; The Self-Driven Child; Untangled; The Power of Showing Up)
7. **Protect the asset.** Sleep, training, and a clear head are the foundation for everything else, including patience at home. Train now for the life you want at 90. (Outlive; Why We Sleep; Essentialism)
8. **Less but better.** Anything short of a clear yes is a no. Protect deep work, protect evenings, and cut what doesn't pay off. (Essentialism; Deep Work; Profit First)
9. **Obstacles are data.** A missed habit, a fight, or a bad week is information about the system. Separate fact from story, then adjust. (Above the Chaos Ex. 2 and 6)
10. **Curiosity before advice.** People are creative, resourceful, and whole, and the best answers are usually already theirs. Ask first, draft second, advise last. (Co-Active Coaching; The Coaching Habit; Coaching for Performance)

## Above the Chaos (Eric Weiss): the operating system

A Stoic framework of nine exercises built on eight principles: focus on what you control, embrace obstacles, practice reflection, cultivate virtue, keep emotional equilibrium, accept the impermanence of outcomes, foster a strong culture, lead by example. Written for leaders; here it's applied to the whole life, where the team is the family. The book is at abovethechaosbook.com, and the full exercise scripts are in the Above the Chaos plugin in this marketplace.

- **Four virtues:** wisdom (sound judgment), courage (doing the uncomfortable necessary thing), justice (fairness and holding yourself accountable), temperance (self-regulation). Temperance is where drinking, screens, and anger live. Courage covers hard conversations and asking for help.
- **Ex. 1 Control and Influence Mapping.** Brain dump, sort into Control, Influence, Beyond Control. Act on one control item, pull one influence lever, pre-decide the response to the worst case. For stress, overwhelm, and avoidance.
- **Ex. 2 Obstacle Transformation.** Separate fact from story, ask what the obstacle reveals, find the opportunity, pick one action. For setbacks, including a broken commitment.
- **Ex. 3 Mindful Journaling.** A daily entry on decisions, how you showed up, and what triggered you. Hand these three prompts to anyone building a journaling habit.
- **Ex. 4 Virtue Assessment.** Score each virtue 1 to 5 on how consistently you live it, drill into the lowest, set one goal per virtue.
- **Ex. 5 Emotional Response Analysis.** Three recent triggers, the most costly one, the common pattern, a designed better response, one simple cue for the pause, and a 30-day end-of-day log. The core tool for anger and reactivity.
- **Ex. 6 Success-Failure Reflection.** How much did it matter 12 months later? For when one bad day, or one good week, distorts judgment.
- **Ex. 7 Culture Audit, at home.** The family culture you want, where it falls short, and your fingerprint on the gaps.
- **Ex. 8 Behavioral Modeling.** The behaviors you want your family to learn by watching you, how consistently you show them, and who will tell you the truth about it.
- **Ex. 9 Philosophy Canvas.** Principles, observable behaviors, and failure modes under pressure. The identity statements from intake are the start of this canvas; the failure modes make it a diagnostic tool.
- **Voice:** direct, grounded, short sentences, ancient wisdom made practical. Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Seneca, amor fati, memento mori, premeditatio malorum.

**Signature questions:** What here is actually in your control? What's the fact and what's the story? What did you do today that you'd be proud for your kids to see?

## Seneca, On Anger

- Anger is a brief madness that does more damage than the offense that caused it. It rests on a judgment that you've been wronged, and you can refuse that judgment.
- First movements are involuntary; acting on them is a choice. The work happens between the impression and the assent.
- Delay is the greatest remedy. Don't act or speak while it's hot.
- Remove the fuel: fatigue, hunger, and too much to do make you irritable. Sleep and alcohol matter here. Avoid hard conversations when depleted.
- The evening review: Seneca examined each day before sleep. Where was I angry, what did I do, what will I do next time. It fits inside a journaling habit.

**Coach moves:** after a blowup, ask what judgment sat under the anger and whether it was true. Before a known trigger, ask what state they're walking in with: tired, hungry, drinking, overloaded.
**Signature question:** What did you assent to that you could have let pass?

## Atomic Habits (James Clear)

- **Identity-based habits.** Outcomes, then processes, then identity. Change starts at identity, and every repetition is evidence for it.
- **The habit loop:** cue, craving, response, reward.
- **Four laws to build a habit:** make it obvious (implementation intentions: "I will do X at time Y in place Z"; habit stacking: "after current habit, new habit"), make it attractive (temptation bundling, join a group where it's normal), make it easy (two-minute rule, cut friction, prepare the environment), make it satisfying (track it, reward it right away).
- **Inversion to break a habit:** make it invisible, unattractive, hard, and unsatisfying. Commitment devices and accountability.
- **Environment design:** phone charging outside the bedroom, no alcohol in the house, the guitar on a stand where you sit.
- **Two-minute rule on hard days:** on a travel day, ten pushups still counts as showing up.
- **Never miss twice.** One miss is an accident; two is the start of a new habit.
- **Plateau of latent potential.** Results lag behavior. Keep going through the flat part.

**Use:** diagnose every slipping habit against the four laws before anyone talks about discipline. Is the cue missing, is it too hard, is the bad option too easy?

**Tiny Habits (BJ Fogg), one tool only:** behavior happens when motivation, ability, and a prompt meet. Anchor each habit to a moment that already happens ("after I park the car at home, the phone goes in the drawer") and celebrate the second it's done.

## The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work (John Gottman)

- **The Four Horsemen and antidotes:** criticism, answered with a gentle startup (complain about the situation, not the person: I feel, about what, I need); contempt, answered with a culture of appreciation; defensiveness, answered with taking responsibility for your part; stonewalling, answered with physiological self-soothing. Going cold after being triggered is stonewalling.
- **Flooding.** When the body is flooded, you can't listen or think. Call a break of at least 20 minutes, do something calming, don't rehearse the grievance, then come back. Agree on the break signal in advance.
- **Bids for connection.** Small reaches for attention. Turning toward builds trust; turning away or against drains it.
- **Accept influence.** Gottman's research found marriages do better when husbands accept influence from their wives. The principle holds for any partner: let their views shape your decisions.
- **Repair attempts.** Any move that de-escalates during or after conflict. Strong couples aren't conflict-free; they repair early.
- **Five to one.** Stable couples keep about five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict.
- **Love maps, fondness and admiration, shared meaning.** Know your partner's inner world, say what you appreciate, build rituals that mean something.
- **Perpetual problems.** Most conflicts are ongoing differences. The goal is to talk about them without the horsemen, not to solve them.

**Eight Dates (John and Julie Gottman):** one structured conversation per date: trust and commitment, conflict, sex and intimacy, work and money, family, fun and adventure, growth and spirituality, dreams. Offer one topic with two or three open questions to bring.

**Signature questions:** Which horseman showed up this week? What bid did you miss? What was your repair?

## Nonviolent Communication (Marshall Rosenberg)

- **Four steps:** observation (what happened, without judgment), feeling, need, request (specific and doable, not a demand).
- **Thoughts dressed as feelings:** "I feel ignored" or "I feel disrespected" are judgments about the other person. Name the feeling under them: hurt, lonely, worried.
- **Anger points to an unmet need.** Stop, breathe, notice the judgment in your head, find the need under it, then speak from the need.
- **Empathy first.** Hear their feeling and need before stating yours.

**Use:** script the hard moment in advance: "When X happened, I felt Y, because I need Z. Would you be willing to W?" After a heated moment, rewrite it in these four parts and rehearse it for next time.

## Come Together (Emily Nagoski)

- **Spontaneous and responsive desire.** Some people feel desire out of the blue; many, especially in long relationships, feel it only once things get started. Both are normal.
- **Brakes and accelerators.** Arousal depends on turning on the accelerators and turning off the brakes. Stress, resentment, and distance are brakes.
- **Context matters more than drive.** Trust, friendship, and how the week went set the conditions.
- **What lasting couples share:** they're friends who trust each other, they make sex a priority, and they focus on pleasure rather than frequency. Planning time for it is fine; waiting for it to happen on its own often means it doesn't.

**Use, as a question to explore:** if one partner tends toward responsive desire, waiting for them to signal wanting first means it rarely starts, and pulling away after conflict presses the brakes for both. Repairing first and pursuing within a good week changes the context. When a pursue habit is missed, ask what got in the way: fear of rejection, unresolved friction, or no time.

## The Self-Driven Child (William Stixrud and Ned Johnson)

- **A sense of control is the antidote to stress,** for kids and adults. Kids who feel in charge of their lives are more motivated and less anxious.
- **Parent as consultant, not boss or manager.** Advise, inform, and support; let them own the decisions that are theirs.
- **It's your call.** Hand over decisions with your input attached, and let natural consequences teach, except where safety is at stake.
- **Nonanxious presence.** A calm parent is the most protective thing a child has. Your stress spreads to them, and so does your calm.
- **Radical downtime and sleep.** Unstructured time and real sleep are part of healthy development.
- **Inner motivation** comes from autonomy, competence, and connection, not pressure.

**Use:** in any conflict with a child, ask whose decision it is. If it's theirs, consult. If it's a call a co-parent leads, back them.
**Signature questions:** Whose decision is this? Did you stay calm while they figured it out?

## Untangled (Lisa Damour)

- Seven transitions for teenage girls: parting with childhood, joining a new tribe, harnessing emotions, contending with adult authority, planning for the future, entering the romantic world, and caring for herself.
- Pulling away from parents is normal development, not rejection. Don't take it personally.
- When she unloads strong feelings, listen first. Ask whether she wants help or just to be heard.

**Use:** for parents of daughters from about 11 on. Name which transition is in play before reacting.

## The Power of Showing Up (Daniel Siegel and Tina Payne Bryson)

- Kids thrive when they feel **safe, seen, soothed, and secure.** Security grows from repeated experiences of the first three.
- **Showing up means presence:** consistent, attuned attention matters more than perfect parenting.
- **Rupture and repair.** Every parent ruptures. What protects the child is coming back, owning it, and reconnecting.

**Use:** after any raised voice with a child, repair the same day: name what you did, apologize, reconnect.

## Outlive (Peter Attia)

- **Medicine 3.0:** prevent the chronic diseases (heart disease, cancer, neurodegenerative disease, metabolic disease) decades early.
- **Exercise is the most powerful longevity tool,** built on four pillars: aerobic base (zone 2), peak aerobic capacity (VO2 max), strength (grip, carries, hinges), and stability.
- **The centenarian decathlon.** List the physical things you want to do in your last decade (hike, carry groceries, get up off the floor, play with grandkids), and train backward from them now.
- **Nutrition, sleep, and emotional health** are the other levers. Attia closes the book on his own emotional health, arguing a long life is worth little without it.

**Use:** an exercise habit should cover all four pillars across the week. When workouts slip, ask what's in their decathlon. For medical questions, point them to their doctor.

## This Naked Mind (Annie Grace)

- Change the beliefs about alcohol, not just the behavior. Examine what you believe it does for you (relaxes you, makes the evening fun, helps you connect, makes an occasion special) and test each belief against how you actually feel the next day.
- Less willpower, more awareness: notice the moment you want a drink and what you expect it to give you.

**Use:** when a drinking commitment breaks, ask what they expected the drink to do and whether it did. If drinking feels out of their control, suggest professional support.

## Why We Sleep (Matthew Walker)

- Keep a regular sleep and wake time, weekends included, a cool and dark room, and caffeine early in the day.
- Alcohol sedates but fragments sleep and suppresses REM, so drinking nights cost the next day.
- Screens and bright light late delay sleep.
- Some of the book's stronger claims have been disputed by other researchers; use it for the basics.

**Use:** a consistent bedtime and the alcohol and screens rules are usually the biggest sleep levers.

## Deep Work and Digital Minimalism (Cal Newport)

- **Deep work** is focused work on hard things, and it's where value gets created. Four rules: work deeply, embrace boredom, quit social media (keep only tools with major benefits), drain the shallows.
- **Rituals.** A set time, place, and rule for focused blocks.
- **Shutdown ritual.** End the workday with a set routine that closes open loops, then stop. It's the bridge to a screens-down evening.
- **Digital declutter.** Step away from optional technology for 30 days, then add back only what serves something you deeply value.
- **High-quality leisure.** Replace passive screen time with demanding leisure: craft, physical activity, real time with people.
- **Solitude.** Time alone with your own thoughts, without inputs.

**Signature question:** Did your first block go to what matters most, and did you shut down on time?

## Essentialism (Greg McKeown)

- Less but better. Explore, eliminate, then execute.
- If it isn't a clear yes, it's a no. The 90 percent rule: rate an opportunity 0 to 100, and anything below 90 is a no.
- Every yes is a trade-off, often against family time or the most important work. Name what it costs.
- Say no gracefully, and protect the asset: sleep and recovery.

**Use:** before any new commitment, a 24-hour pause and the 90 percent rule.

## Profit First (Mike Michalowicz)

- Take profit first: sales minus profit equals expenses. Allocate every deposit to accounts for profit, owner's pay, tax, and operating expenses, each with a target percentage.
- Smaller operating accounts force leaner spending.
- Allocate on a fixed rhythm, twice a month, and distribute profit each quarter.

**Use:** for business owners who add Money as an area. For anyone else, a weekly money review habit does the same job. Not financial advice; for specific decisions, point them to a professional.
