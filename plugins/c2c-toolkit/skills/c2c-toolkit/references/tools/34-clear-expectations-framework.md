# CLEAR EXPECTATIONS FRAMEWORK
Author: Eric Weiss  
Purpose: Set Crystal-Clear Delegation Expectations So Founders Can Stop Being The Bottleneck  
Audience: Founders, CEOs, Executives, Managers  

---

# 1. PURPOSE OF THIS TOOL

Every scaling founder hits the same wall. They know they cannot do everything themselves, but they keep stepping in, redoing work, and micromanaging the team.

The excuses sound reasonable:

- "No one can do it as well as I can."
- "I've tried delegating, but things fall through the cracks."
- "It takes longer to explain than to just do it myself."

These are not delegation problems. They are expectation problems.

Most delegation fails because the expectations were too vague. Tasks get assigned without clear outcomes, success criteria, or ownership. People interpret things differently, make the wrong calls, and lose confidence in their authority. The founder steps back in, the cycle reinforces, and the belief calcifies: "It's just easier to do it myself."

People do not fail because they are incapable. They fail because they were not set up to succeed.

This tool provides the structural fix: the CLEAR framework. It does not address the founder's psychological resistance to letting go (that's Tool #25 Identity Ceiling). It addresses the mechanics of the handoff itself. Use them together when both layers are blocked.

---

# 2. WHEN TO USE

Use when:

- Founder is doing work the team should be doing
- Delegated work keeps coming back wrong
- The founder rewrites or redoes team output
- Team members ask repeated clarifying questions
- "Just do it the way I would" is a recurring instruction
- New hires take months to make decisions independently
- Cross-functional projects fall apart at the handoff
- The founder is exhausted but refuses to release work

If the founder says "I can't trust my team," the test is: have they given the team what they need to succeed? Run this tool to find out.

---

# 3. THE CORE INSIGHT

Delegation is not "tell someone what to do." Delegation is "transfer ownership of an outcome with everything required to succeed."

Most founders give instructions. CLEAR gives ownership.

The difference is whether the person on the receiving end can execute without coming back to the founder. If they can, you delegated. If they can't, you assigned.

---

# 4. THE CLEAR FRAMEWORK

Five components. Every delegation conversation must hit all five. Skipping one is the failure mode.

## C — Context: Why does this matter?

Most founders assume the team knows why a task is important. They don't. Without context, people make decisions in the dark and produce work that misses the point.

❌ Vague: "We need a new onboarding flow."

✅ CLEAR: "Our current onboarding causes 40% of trial users to drop off. We need a smoother experience to increase conversions."

Context is the difference between a person executing a task and a person solving a problem. Solvers make better calls. Executors need constant supervision.

## L — Limitations: What constraints must be followed?

"Use common sense" is not a constraint. If there are real limits on budget, technology, timeline, or scope, name them before work begins.

❌ Vague: "Design a new landing page."

✅ CLEAR: "Design a new landing page using our existing brand colors and the current Webflow CMS. Budget is $5K. Live by next Friday."

Unstated constraints become rework. Every founder who has said "oh I should have mentioned" knows the cost.

## E — Expectations: What does "good" look like?

Most delegation fails because leaders give tasks instead of outcomes. Define what "done" means in observable terms.

❌ Vague: "Fix the login bug."

✅ CLEAR: "Users can log in consistently across all devices. Must pass testing on Chrome, Safari, and Firefox. Response time under 1 second."

If you cannot describe what success looks like, you cannot delegate it. The clearer the outcome, the more autonomy the team can hold.

## A — Accountability: Who owns what?

When multiple people touch a project, ownership gets diluted. If no one is explicitly responsible, the work falls through the cracks.

❌ Vague: "Marketing and Sales need to figure out a campaign."

✅ CLEAR: "Jamie (CMO) owns campaign strategy. Alex (Head of Sales) provides feedback to ensure alignment with sales needs. Jamie has the final call."

One owner per outcome. Collaborators are named explicitly. Decision rights are stated. No more finger-pointing when something breaks.

## R — Review: How will we track progress?

The founder who delegates and disappears is not delegating, they are abandoning. The founder who checks in every day is not delegating, they are micromanaging. The fix is structured checkpoints.

❌ Vague: "Get this done by Friday."

✅ CLEAR: "Wednesday: rough draft check-in so we can course-correct. Friday: final delivery."

Checkpoints reduce surprise, allow mid-course correction, and give the team room to execute between them.

# 5. THE COMPLETE WORKSHEET

Use this template for every meaningful delegation. Yes, every one.

| Step | Guiding Questions |
|---|---|
| **C — Context** | Why does this matter? What problem are we solving? What's the bigger picture? |
| **L — Limitations** | What constraints must be followed? Budget, timeline, tools, scope, non-negotiables? |
| **E — Expectations** | What does success look like? What measurable outcomes? What must be true for "done"? |
| **A — Accountability** | Who owns delivery? Who else is involved? What are their specific roles? |
| **R — Review** | How will we track progress? What checkpoints? When and how is feedback given? |

A delegation conversation should produce a written CLEAR doc. Slack message, Notion page, email, doesn't matter. Written. Both parties have the same reference. No "I thought you said" later.

# 6. WORKED EXAMPLES

These show what CLEAR looks like in real scenarios. Use them as templates.

## Example 1: Investor Update (CEO → Chief of Staff)

**Vague:** "Put together an investor update for next week."

**Context:** Investors need a clear picture of progress, financials, and plans to maintain confidence.

**Limitations:** Max 2 pages. Must include revenue and runway. Use existing template.

**Expectations:**
- Sections: wins, revenue growth, product, hiring, next steps
- Quarterly financials and runway projection included
- Bullet points only, no long paragraphs
- Final to CEO by Thursday EOD

**Accountability:** Chief of Staff owns the draft. CFO reviews financials before CEO sees it.

**Review:** Final version in Google Doc by Thursday night. CEO approves. Sent to investors Friday morning.

## Example 2: Product Roadmap (CEO → Head of Product)

**Vague:** "We need a better product roadmap."

**Context:** Roadmap is too reactive. Need a 12-month vision to align team and investors.

**Limitations:** Next 4 quarters. Only features that drive revenue or retention. Notion or Miro format.

**Expectations:**
- 3-5 major themes for the year
- Top customer pain points driving churn identified
- Nice-to-have backlog separated from must-haves
- Sales and CS feedback incorporated before finalizing

**Accountability:** Head of Product owns the roadmap. Check in with CEO after customer research is complete.

**Review:** Draft Friday. Finalized in Monday exec meeting.

## Example 3: Marketing Campaign (CEO → VP Marketing)

**Vague:** "We need a stronger online presence. Let's do a campaign."

**Context:** Losing deals because prospects don't know the brand. Need targeted awareness and lead generation.

**Limitations:** $25K budget. LinkedIn and YouTube only. Launch by next quarter.

**Expectations:**
- 3 key content pieces (video, blog, LinkedIn post)
- Track engagement, clicks, conversion rates
- Customer testimonials or case studies included

**Accountability:** VP Marketing owns the campaign. Coordinates with Sales for lead handoff.

**Review:** Campaign plan presented in next week's leadership meeting for approval.

## Example 4: Fundraising Deck (CEO → CFO + Designer)

**Vague:** "We need a pitch deck for our next round."

**Context:** Preparing for $10M Series A. Need a compelling deck that tells a clear story.

**Limitations:** Max 12 slides. Focus on market, traction, revenue model, differentiation. Minimal text, strong visuals.

**Expectations:**
- CFO finalizes revenue projections and valuation assumptions
- Designer makes slides visually engaging while keeping it simple
- 3 strong customer success stories included

**Accountability:** CFO owns financials. Designer owns visuals. CEO owns story and narrative.

**Review:** Draft next Monday. Joint review session. Final by following Friday.

## Example 5: VP of Sales Hire (CEO → Recruiter + Hiring Manager)

**Vague:** "We need to hire a great VP of Sales ASAP."

**Context:** Struggling to close deals. Need a VP of Sales to build a scalable process and grow revenue.

**Limitations:** $180K-$220K base plus equity. SaaS sales leadership, 5+ years managing teams. Offer made within 90 days.

**Expectations:**
- Recruiter shortlists 5 strong candidates within 3 weeks
- Hiring Manager runs structured interviews with clear scorecards
- Final candidates complete a take-home assignment

**Accountability:** Recruiter sources. Hiring Manager screens. CEO interviews final 2.

**Review:** Weekly progress update with expected close date.

# 7. RUN OF SHOW: RUNNING CLEAR ON A REAL DELEGATION

When using this tool with a founder for the first time, walk through one of their actual pending delegations live. Do not lecture the framework. Apply it.

| Step | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Pick a real task | 5 min | Founder names something they are about to delegate or have delegated badly |
| 2. Context check | 5 min | Articulate why it matters in one sentence |
| 3. Limitations check | 5 min | List every constraint, including the unstated ones |
| 4. Expectations check | 10 min | Define done. Push past vague language |
| 5. Accountability check | 5 min | One owner. Named collaborators. Stated decision rights |
| 6. Review check | 5 min | Schedule the checkpoints into a calendar invite during the session |
| 7. Write the CLEAR doc | 10 min | You write it. A peer, advisor, or AI pressure-tests it |
| 8. Send it | 5 min | Delivered to the owner before the session ends |

Total: 45-50 minutes for one delegation. The first one is slow. By the third, founders run it in 10 minutes.

# 8. THE FOUNDER FAILURE MODES

Watch for:

- Skipping Context because "they should know"
- Listing aspirational constraints instead of real ones
- Defining Expectations as activities instead of outcomes ("write a draft" vs "draft that includes X, Y, Z and passes the bar of W")
- Naming a team instead of an owner under Accountability
- Setting Review as "let me know when it's done" (that's not a checkpoint, that's an ending)
- Writing the CLEAR doc and then bypassing it in real-time conversations
- Using CLEAR to micromanage by over-specifying Expectations down to the keystroke
- Delegating CLEAR itself ("here, you fill out the framework")

The most common failure: founders write CLEAR docs that look good and then immediately undermine them by jumping in mid-execution. The framework only works if the founder respects the Review checkpoints and stays out between them.

---

# 9. WHEN CLEAR IS NOT THE FIX

CLEAR fixes the mechanics of delegation. It does not fix:

- A founder who cannot psychologically let go (route to Tool #25 Identity Ceiling)
- A team without the skills to execute the work (route to Tool #28 Executive Hiring Clarity)
- Unclear authority over who can decide what (route to Tool #24 Authority Audit)
- A team that does not know how to weigh trade-offs (route to Tool #33 Core Values Decision Framework)
- Tolerated mediocrity from a specific person who never delivers (route to Tool #22 Toleration Audit)

If the founder runs CLEAR cleanly and the work still comes back wrong, the diagnosis is deeper than expectations. Re-route.

---

# 10. HOW TO RUN IT WELL

When you run this tool:

1. Start with one real, current delegation. Not a hypothetical.
2. Write the Context in one sentence. If they cannot, the delegation is not ready.
3. Hunt for unstated constraints. Ask "what would frustrate you if they did it that way?" Every answer is a hidden Limitation.
4. Push hard on Expectations. Most founders say "use good judgment" because they have not done the work to define done.
5. Name a single owner. Not a team. Not a department. A person.
6. Put the Review checkpoints on the calendar right away.
7. Write the CLEAR doc yourself, then have a peer, advisor, or AI pressure-test it.
8. Send it the same day. Do not let it sit in drafts.
9. Schedule a 7-day check to review whether the delegation held without you stepping in.
10. If you rewrite or redo the delegated work despite a clean CLEAR doc, the issue is identity, not mechanics. Route to Tool #25.

---

# 11. AI IMPLEMENTATION RULES

If an AI helps a founder run CLEAR:

It must:

1. Ask the founder to name a specific real delegation, not a category
2. Walk through C-L-E-A-R sequentially, one component at a time
3. Reject vague Context with "Why does this matter?" until a specific answer emerges
4. Generate likely hidden Limitations by asking about budget, tools, timeline, stakeholders
5. Force Expectations into observable, testable outcomes
6. Reject "the team" as an answer for Accountability
7. Insist on real calendar dates for Review checkpoints
8. Output a clean CLEAR doc the founder can paste into Slack or email

It must not:

- Generate generic delegation advice
- Let the founder skip components
- Accept "they should know" as Context
- Treat Review as an ending date instead of a series of checkpoints
- Write the CLEAR doc instead of guiding the founder to write it

Tone: Direct, structural, allergic to vagueness. Treat fuzzy language as the enemy.

# 13. THE ONE QUESTION

Before stepping in to redo delegated work, the founder asks:

**"Have I given them what they need to succeed, or am I about to punish them for my own failure to set expectations?"**

If the answer is the second one, the fix is CLEAR. Not micromanagement.
