# CORE VALUES DECISION FRAMEWORK
Author: Eric Weiss  
Purpose: Turn Core Values Into a Decision-Making System That Eliminates Leadership Bottlenecks  
Audience: Founders, CEOs, CTOs, Engineering Managers, Team Leads  

---

# 1. PURPOSE OF THIS TOOL

Most leaders are bottlenecks. Every decision flows up to them. Their teams cannot make independent calls that align with the company's direction, so the leader spends their day clarifying, course-correcting, and approving things their team should be handling alone.

Why it happens:

- Teams have no shared framework for making decisions
- Company values exist but live on a wall, not in daily work
- Decisions are inconsistent because there is no agreement on when to prioritize speed vs quality, transparency vs autonomy
- Different people on the same team have different priorities and risk tolerances

The result is reactive leadership. The leader becomes the single point of failure for decisions the team is fully capable of making themselves.

Most "core values" exercises produce fluff that nobody uses. This tool fixes that by turning values into an operational decision-making system, with trade-off rules, behavioral examples, a team charter, and a feedback and performance loop that keeps the values alive.

This is distinct from Tool #20 (Authority Audit), which fixes who owns what. This tool fixes how owners decide once authority is clear.

---

# 2. WHEN TO USE

Use when:

- The leader is approving decisions the team should be making
- Team members make inconsistent calls in similar situations
- Trade-offs (speed vs quality, autonomy vs transparency) cause recurring debate
- The company has values posted but they do not show up in daily work
- A new team is forming and needs a shared decision system
- Two teams within the same company make decisions that contradict each other
- Performance reviews feel arbitrary because there is no rubric
- Onboarding a new hire and you want them to make aligned decisions on day 30, not day 180

If the leader is the bottleneck and authority rights are already clear, this is the tool.

---

# 3. THE CORE INSIGHT

Values are not statements. Values are decision rules.

A team that says "we value quality" is doing nothing. A team that says "quality beats speed when shipping core infrastructure, and speed beats quality when running a beta experiment" has a system.

The job of this tool is to translate aspirational values into context-specific trade-off rules, then translate those rules into observable behaviors, then embed those behaviors into the rituals where culture is actually built: feedback, recognition, and performance review.

---

# 4. STEP 1: IDENTIFY CORE VALUES

Before anything else, the leader must clarify what values actually mean in this team's context. Not what sounds good. What is true.

## Diagnostic Questions

Answer all of these. Be specific. Use real examples from the last 90 days.

1. What do you reward and recognize on this team?
2. What behaviors frustrate you the most? (These point to values being violated.)
3. What trade-offs are you consistently making? (Where do you reliably choose A over B?)
4. What behaviors lead to success in this company?
5. What are the non-negotiables? (What must always be upheld, regardless of context?)

## Output

A list of 3 to 5 core values. Each with a one-sentence definition that explains what it means in practice.

Example for a tech leader:

- **Quality** — We strive for excellence in everything we build.
- **Speed** — We move fast and iterate quickly.
- **Transparency** — We communicate openly and share knowledge freely.
- **Autonomy** — We empower individuals to own decisions and outcomes.

If the definition is vague, the value is useless. Push for specificity.

---

# 5. STEP 2: TRANSLATE VALUES INTO TRADE-OFF RULES

This is the step everyone skips. It is also the step that makes the framework actually work.

Values contradict each other. Quality slows speed. Transparency slows autonomy. The team needs explicit rules for which value wins in which context.

## Build a Trade-Off Matrix

For each pair of values that can conflict, write a contextual rule.

Format:
**Value A > Value B when [context]**
**Value B > Value A when [context]**

## Example Trade-Off Rules

- Quality > Speed when launching a core infrastructure update
- Speed > Quality when experimenting with a new feature in a limited beta
- Transparency > Autonomy when making decisions that affect the entire organization
- Autonomy > Transparency when a team needs to move fast without waiting for consensus

## The Test

Apply the rules to two real decisions the team faced recently. Did the rules produce the same answer the leader would have given? If yes, the rules are working. If no, either the rules need refinement or the leader's instincts were inconsistent. Either is useful information.

---

# 6. STEP 3: ALIGN INDIVIDUAL AND TEAM VALUES

Each team member runs the same exercise on themselves. Then compare.

## Individual Reflection

Each person answers:

1. What values matter most to me in my work?
2. How do those values align with company values?
3. Where do they contradict?

## Surface the Contradictions

Common examples:

- A designer who values creativity on a team that prioritizes speed
- A backend engineer who values stability on a team that prioritizes autonomy and fast deploys
- A PM who values consensus on a team that prioritizes decisive action

Do not force alignment. Do not pretend the contradictions do not exist. Discuss them openly. The goal is for each person to understand where they will need to compromise and where the team will accommodate them.

This conversation alone resolves a huge amount of latent friction.

---

# 7. STEP 4: DEFINE GOOD AND BAD BEHAVIORS

Values stay abstract until they are tied to observable behaviors. This is the alignment exercise.

For each core value, the team builds three lists together:

1. **Aligned Behaviors** — What does living this value look like?
2. **Misaligned Behaviors** — What does violating this value look like?
3. **Principles and Commitments** — What does the team agree to?

## Example: Autonomy

**Aligned Behaviors:**
- Engineers deploy their own code after peer review
- PMs make prioritization decisions without leadership approval for every feature
- Designers ship minor UI updates without extensive sign-offs

**Misaligned Behaviors:**
- Every major decision goes through a committee, causing delays
- Engineers need managerial approval to push minor bug fixes
- Designers cannot test new UX without full stakeholder alignment

**Principles and Commitments:**
- We trust individuals to make informed decisions in their areas of expertise
- We minimize unnecessary approvals while keeping accountability for outcomes
- We give teams the access and information they need to move fast

## Example: Transparency

**Aligned Behaviors:**
- Product teams share decision-making rationale openly
- Engineers document technical trade-offs and make decisions visible
- Designers post UX updates in a shared channel for discussion

**Misaligned Behaviors:**
- Product changes are made without explaining why to engineering
- Engineers fix bugs without logging them, creating knowledge gaps
- Designers silo feedback, leading to misalignment

**Principles and Commitments:**
- We communicate decisions, risks, and rationale proactively
- We default to open discussion over private conversations
- Critical knowledge is shared, not locked in individual minds

## Example: Quality

**Aligned Behaviors:**
- Engineers prioritize tests and clean code
- PMs push back on rushing features that are not ready
- Designers conduct user testing before significant UX changes

**Misaligned Behaviors:**
- Engineers cut corners to hit a deadline, creating tech debt
- PMs release features without validating customer impact
- Designers prioritize aesthetics over usability

**Principles and Commitments:**
- We prioritize long-term maintainability over short-term speed
- We validate major decisions with real-world feedback
- We hold high standards but recognize when "good enough" is appropriate

## Example: Speed

**Aligned Behaviors:**
- Engineers ship MVPs quickly and iterate based on user feedback
- PMs deliver incremental value rather than perfecting before launch
- Designers test multiple versions of a UI rather than over-researching

**Misaligned Behaviors:**
- Engineers over-optimize code before knowing if a feature will be used
- PMs delay releases to add "just one more feature"
- Designers polish mockups without user validation

**Principles and Commitments:**
- We optimize for learning and iteration rather than perfection
- We bias toward action and adapt based on new insights
- We move fast while balancing risk and impact

## The Rule

If a team cannot generate concrete examples for a value, the value is fake. Either redefine it or remove it.

---

# 8. STEP 5: CODIFY INTO A TEAM CHARTER

Once behaviors are defined, codify everything into a single living document.

## Charter Outline

1. **Core Values** — Defined and prioritized
2. **Trade-Off Rules** — When does each value win?
3. **Aligned Behaviors** — Real examples per value
4. **Misaligned Behaviors** — Real examples per value
5. **Principles and Commitments** — Concrete decision-making statements

## Sample Commitments

- We ship fast when quality is not mission-critical
- We default to transparency in all decisions affecting the team
- We trust individuals to deploy code in their domain after peer review
- We escalate trade-offs to the team when they conflict with charter rules

The charter is not a poster. It is the reference document a team member opens when they are about to make a hard call and want to know how the team would weigh it.

A charter that lives in a Notion page nobody opens is dead. The charter must be:

- Linked from the team's primary workspace
- Reviewed in onboarding for every new hire
- Updated whenever a new trade-off pattern emerges
- Referenced explicitly in feedback, retros, and reviews

---

# 9. STEP 6: EMBED VALUES INTO RITUALS

This is where the framework becomes culture. The values must show up in the meetings, feedback loops, and recognition systems where the team actually spends time.

## In Meetings

**Retrospectives** — Use values as a lens.
"Did we balance speed vs quality this sprint? Where did we lean too far?"

**Sprint Planning** — Ask how upcoming work aligns.
"How does this prioritize transparency and autonomy?"

**Standups** — Reinforce commitments.
"I noticed great transparency in how the tech debt got documented last sprint. Keep that up."

## In One-on-One Feedback

Always tie feedback to a specific value.

**Recognition:**
"I appreciated how you prioritized transparency by explaining the trade-offs in the meeting. It helped everyone align faster."

**Correction:**
"When you bypassed peer review, that compromised our commitment to quality. How can we balance speed with quality better next time?"

Generic feedback is forgettable. Value-anchored feedback teaches the system.

## In Performance Reviews

Use a values rubric.

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 5 | Consistently embodies core values and reinforces them in others |
| 4 | Frequently aligns with core values, room for improvement |
| 3 | Sometimes aligns, needs more consistency |
| 2 | Occasionally misaligned, requires improvement |
| 1 | Frequently misaligned, impacting team effectiveness |

Rate every team member on every value. Use the behaviors lists from Step 4 as evidence. No more "I just feel like they are not a great fit."

## In Public Recognition

**Value Call-Outs:**
At all-hands or team meetings, name people who embody values.
"Shout-out to Alex for embodying autonomy by owning the incident response without needing approvals."

**Team Awards:**
Quarterly "Values Champion" award. Peer-nominated. Tie it to bonuses or visibility if you want it to land.

What gets recognized gets repeated.

# 10. THE RUN OF SHOW

For a leader installing this framework with their team for the first time:

| Session | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Session 1 (solo) | 60 min | Leader runs Step 1, drafts values |
| Session 2 (solo) | 60 min | Leader builds trade-off matrix from Step 2 |
| Session 3 (team) | 90 min | Team alignment exercise, individual values from Step 3 |
| Session 4 (team) | 2 hours | Behavioral examples per value from Step 4 |
| Session 5 (solo) | 60 min | Leader codifies charter from Step 5 |
| Session 6 (team) | 60 min | Charter review, refinement, commitment |
| Ongoing | — | Embed into rituals from Step 6 |

Total elapsed time: roughly 2 weeks if running parallel, 4 weeks if sequential. Treat it as a project, not a meeting.

---

# 11. FAILURE MODES

Watch for:

- Values that sound impressive but generate no behavioral examples
- A trade-off matrix that has no real conflicts (the values do not actually compete, which means they are not load-bearing)
- A charter that gets written and never opened again
- Feedback that does not reference values
- Performance reviews that ignore the rubric and revert to gut feel
- The leader who built the framework still being the bottleneck three months later
- Teams using values as a weapon to attack each other instead of as a shared standard
- Values copied from another company without contextualizing

If the leader is still approving the same decisions six months later, the framework was not embedded. It was performed.

---

# 12. HOW TO RUN IT WELL

How to run it well:

1. Diagnose the bottleneck first. Confirm the leader is actually a decision bottleneck before installing this. If the issue is unclear authority, route to Tool #20 instead.
2. Draft the values alone before bringing the team in. Your instincts are the ground truth.
3. Force the trade-off matrix. This is where most teams want to skip. Do not let them.
4. For the first team session, consider an outside facilitator (an advisor, a peer founder, or an AI preparing you) so you can take part instead of steering.
5. Insist on real examples from the last 90 days for every behavior list. Hypotheticals do not count.
6. Draft the charter, then have the team revise it themselves. They will not own it if they did not write it.
7. Put a 60-day check on your calendar. Verify that feedback and retros are actually using value language.
8. At 90 days, ask yourself: "What decisions am I no longer being pulled into?" If the answer is "none," the framework failed and you need to diagnose why.

---

# 13. AI IMPLEMENTATION RULES

If an AI helps a leader run this framework:

It must:

1. Start by confirming the leader is a decision bottleneck
2. Walk the leader through the diagnostic questions in Step 1 one at a time
3. Push back on vague value definitions
4. Force the leader to generate real trade-off rules with explicit context
5. Generate behavioral examples by asking about specific recent situations
6. Help draft the charter as a structured document
7. Suggest specific feedback language and meeting prompts
8. Output a usable team charter at the end

It must not:

- Generate generic "core values" lists that sound like every other company
- Skip the trade-off matrix
- Accept aspirational behaviors without grounding them in real examples
- Let the leader off the hook on contradictions
- Treat values as a branding exercise

Tone: Direct, structured, allergic to corporate fluff. If the leader produces "we value excellence," push back hard. Excellence is not a value. It is a placeholder for one.

# 15. THE ONE QUESTION

When the leader is tempted to weigh in on a decision the team should be making, ask:

**"What does our charter say about this trade-off, and why is the team coming to me instead of resolving it themselves?"**

If the answer is "the charter does not cover it," update the charter.
If the answer is "the team did not consult the charter," reinforce the ritual.
If the answer is "the team is afraid to decide," that is a different problem and the framework cannot solve it alone.
