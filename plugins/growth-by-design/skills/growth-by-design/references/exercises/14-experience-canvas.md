## Why it matters

A working solution grabs attention. A great experience earns loyalty. Features get copied and prices press downward, so what keeps customers is how it feels to use your product. Linear is the book's example: in a market full of capable project management tools, it won on the experience itself, every interaction fast and every detail considered. When capability is commoditized, experience becomes the moat.

The experience starts long before the first click. Map four phases: Awareness (they realize the problem, and your message makes a promise the rest of the journey must keep), Consideration (they compare alternatives), Decision (they shortlist, test, buy, and onboard, where the product must deliver on the promise), and Goal accomplishment (they solve the problem, and you give them a reason to return, upgrade, and bring others). Onboarding begins the instant they hear about you, so the path to the Aha Moment starts at awareness.

The Aha Moment is when a customer goes from "I'm listening" to "I get it." The faster and easier you get them there, the more likely they stay and pay. That's the entire job of onboarding. A Point of Friction makes them think, decide, or work, so defer it until after the Aha Moment. A Point of Departure is worse: it forces them to leave and come back (fetching a card, waiting for a verification email), and you may lose them. Cut what you ask for and teach to the bone, and show instead of tell, the way video games drop in one skill at the moment you need it. Then celebrate the win. Instacart shows a screen after every order telling you how many hours you've saved shopping, because they charge a premium and make sure you feel the value.

Design principles are the standard: short guardrails in two columns, what it should be and what it should not be. If a feature, screen, or flow doesn't follow them, it doesn't ship.

## Use it when

- Users sign up but don't stick, or churn before reaching value.
- Experience scored under 3, or Experience 4 or 5 (measured satisfaction, mapped journey) scored 1 or 2.
- The team can't agree on how the product should feel.

## Before you start

Ask whether they have a working product or prototype, and any analytics, session replays, or NPS/CSAT data. Ask for the "awesome" language from #4 From Suck to Awesome; it becomes the design principles. If there's no product yet, map the intended path and tag each step [Assumption], so the canvas becomes their prototype test plan for #13. If they have data, use it to mark where people stall. Don't block them either way.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 8: The Aha Moment**
> What's the instant a new customer truly gets it? What are they doing, and what do they see?

Push for: one observable moment tied to the job. Ask: "How would you see it happen in your data?"

**Step 2 of 8: Where the path starts**
> How do people first hear about you, and what does that first touch promise them?

Push for: the real source (a friend, an ad, a landing page) and the exact promise the rest of the path must keep.

**Step 3 of 8: Every step to the Aha**
> Walk me through every step from that first touch to the Aha Moment. Every click, field, choice, and screen.

Push for: granular steps, including digging out a credit card or leaving to sign in elsewhere. Count them. If they skip, ask: "What happens between those two?"

**Step 4 of 8: Mark each step**
> For each step, is it a delight, a friction, or a departure? And is it critical or optional?

Push for: honest marks. Any step that makes them think, decide, or work is friction. Any step that makes them leave is a departure.

**Step 5 of 8: What you ask and what you teach**
> What information and permissions do you ask for before the Aha, and what do you try to teach?

Push for: a full list, then a cut list. Anything not essential to the Aha waits. Permissions need a one-line reason and benefit, or they wait too. Check the path against the book's three flow rules: purpose driven (one goal, a clean definition of done), minimal paths (prune the bush of branches and repetition), and light on the customer (eliminate, defer, or automate every step you can).

**Step 6 of 8: Celebrate the win**
> When they hit the Aha Moment, how do you mark it?

Push for: a moment that restates the benefit they just felt, plus a follow-up email with a next step and a lifeline.

**Step 7 of 8: Design principles, it SHOULD be**
> In their words, what should this feel like? Give me three or four words.

Push for: words from customers' "awesome" language, such as the book's examples "effortless, automated, reliable." Ask: "Which customer said that?"

**Step 8 of 8: Design principles, it should NOT be**
> What should it never feel like?

Push for: the opposites, drawn from what sucks today ("never overcomplicated or rigid"). Then test: "Name one current screen that breaks a principle."

Close by asking how they'll measure it: NPS or CSAT at key touchpoints, and an A/B test on onboarding with each account flagged by version and tracked for retention and conversion.

## Push back when

- **The Aha is vague.** "If you can't see it in the data, you can't shorten the path to it. What do they do at that moment?"
- **The map starts at signup.** "Onboarding starts when they first hear about you. What did they expect before they got here?"
- **A card or profile is required up front.** "That's a departure before they've gotten any value. Can it wait until after the Aha?"
- **Principles are generic.** "'Easy to use' fits every product. What did your customers say?"
- **Nothing gets cut.** The workbook's rule: cut or defer every step that isn't a delight or essential to reaching the Aha Moment.
- **No measurement.** "Don't stab in the dark. Which touchpoint will you score first?"

## Output

```
GROWTH BY DESIGN · Experience Canvas
[Company] · [Date]

The Aha Moment: [observable moment]  [Evidence/Assumption]
The path to it (from first contact):
  1. [step] · [delight / friction / departure] · [critical / optional] · [keep / cut / defer]
  2. ...
  Total steps: [N] · Frictions: [N] · Departures: [N]
Design principles, it SHOULD be: [words]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Design principles, it should NOT be: [words]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Celebrate the win: [how]
Measurement: [NPS/CSAT touchpoints, onboarding A/B test]
```

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the map revealed, for example how many steps sit before the Aha, where the departures are, and whether the principles come from customer language.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] field with the cheapest test, often a prototype session or session replay review.

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "cut or defer the top two frictions and watch three new users go through the new path."

## Done well

- The Aha Moment is observable in data.
- The path starts at awareness and every step is marked.
- Departures before the Aha are gone or have a plan.
- Principles come from customers' words and can veto a design.
