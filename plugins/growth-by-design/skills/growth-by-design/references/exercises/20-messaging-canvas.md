## Why it matters

You can have a great product and a real differentiator and still lose, because if nobody understands why you're better, you may as well not exist. The essence of marketing is talking to someone about their problems in their own language. When a customer reads their own words on your landing page, something clicks: this company gets me. Don't translate their pain into polished ad-speak. If they say "stuff slips through the cracks," the headline says stuff slips through the cracks.

Dollar Shave Club's launch video had the founder talking like a regular guy fed up with overpriced razors, in plain, slightly annoyed customer language. It cost a few thousand dollars, drove tens of thousands of signups in 48 hours, and worked because it sounded like a real person.

Position before you pitch. April Dunford (Obviously Awesome) breaks positioning into the competitive alternatives, your unique attributes, the value they deliver, and the segment that cares most. Get it wrong and customers judge you against the wrong competitors. The most common founder mistake is making the product the hero. Donald Miller (Building a StoryBrand) makes the customer the hero and the product the guide. Alexander Osterwalder's Value Proposition Canvas lines up the customer's jobs, pains, and gains against your pain relievers and gain creators; every "wouldn't it be awesome if..." is a gain waiting to become a value proposition.

Then test the message like a product. Read the copy to a real customer and have them rephrase it. If they say it back the way you meant it, it works. If they stumble or rewrite it, their version is usually better, so use theirs. At scale, A/B test headlines on landing pages and ads. You can even test before the product exists, with a blog post or ad built on their exact words.

## Use it when

- The homepage describes features, or uses words no customer has said.
- Messaging scored under 3, or Messaging 4 (verbatim customer language) is a 1 or 2.
- Prospects need a long explanation before they get it.
- Before #21 Channel Canvas or any ad spend.

## Before you start

This canvas runs on verbatim quotes. Ask for interview notes, reviews, support tickets, or call transcripts, and have them paste raw phrases. Pull from #9 Customer Interview Tracker and #18 Differentiation Canvas if done. With fewer than ten real quotes, say so: the copy will be the founder's words. Draft anyway, tag it [Assumption], and make collecting quotes the next goal.

The skill drafts candidate copy from the founder's quotes. Keep the customer's words intact; resist smoothing them.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 7: Verbatim words**
> Paste the exact phrases customers use for the pain and for the win. Their words, not your summary.

Push for: emotional, specific quotes ("I freaking hate it when..." and "it would be awesome if..."). If they paraphrase, ask: "Who said that, and what exactly did they say?" Sort quotes into pain and win.

**Step 2 of 7: The alternative**
> If you didn't exist, what would this customer use instead?

Push for: what customers actually use today, including workarounds, from #18 if done. This anchors Dunford's competitive alternatives and stops customers from judging you against the wrong competitors.

**Step 3 of 7: Positioning statement**
Draft it using the template, pulling segment and need from #7, category, value, and difference from #18 (ask for any that are missing, one at a time), then ask:
> Does this sound true to you, and which part would a customer push back on?

Push for: one segment, one category, one difference. If the category is invented, ask what customers would search for.

**Step 4 of 7: One-liner**
Draft two or three candidates built from the strongest pain and win quotes, then ask:
> Which of these would a customer repeat to a friend?

Push for: their problem and your fix in one sentence, customer as hero. If every candidate names a feature, rewrite around the outcome.

**Step 5 of 7: Value proposition**
> What outcome do they want, said the way they'd say it?

Push for: the benefit and the outcome, with the cost of the problem in time or money. Map one pain reliever and one gain creator to their quotes.

**Step 6 of 7: Proof**
> What data, results, or testimonials back up the claim?

Push for: real numbers and named customers. If there's none, tag [Assumption] and name the first proof point to collect.

**Step 7 of 7: Rephrase test**
> Who's one real customer you can read the one-liner to this week, and have them say it back in their own words?

Push for: a named person and a date. If they've already done it, capture the customer's exact restatement. If it differs, propose the customer's version as the new one-liner.

## Push back when

- **The copy is polished ad-speak.** "No customer talks like that. Which quote did this come from?"
- **The product is the hero.** "This is about you. Rewrite it so the customer is the one who wins."
- **They lead with features.** "Lead with the outcome. What does their life look like once the problem is gone?"
- **They skip the rephrase test.** "You don't know it works until a customer says it back. Who can you read it to this week?"
- **They defend their version over the customer's.** "Their version is usually better. Use theirs."
- **The workbook's rule:** "If you don't describe your product the way your customers would, rewrite it."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the work revealed: how much copy came from verbatim quotes, whether the customer is the hero, what the rephrase test showed.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] field with the cheapest test (rephrase test, a two-headline A/B test, or a blog post or ad built on their words).

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "read the one-liner to [named customer] and write down exactly what they say back."

## Done well

- The one-liner contains at least one phrase a customer actually said.
- The customer is the hero; the product is the guide.
- A real customer has restated it, or one is booked.
- Proof is specific, or the gap is named.
