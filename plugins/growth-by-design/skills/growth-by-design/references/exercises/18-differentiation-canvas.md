## Why it matters

No one can be the best at everything, so find your 10X factor. Peter Thiel puts it bluntly in Zero to One: to displace an incumbent you need to be ten times better at something, not ten percent. Incremental wins get lost in a crowded category. A 10X difference is the only thing strong enough to break a customer's habit and make them move.

You get there by being the best in the world at one thing for one segment. April Dunford calls this "only-ness" in Obviously Awesome: the value that only you can claim for a defined group. That demands tradeoffs. You deemphasize many things to emphasize the one, and for the customers who care most about it, you become the undeniable winner. Simplest, fastest, most secure, cheapest, highest quality: pick the superpower and go all in.

Customers have already told you what it should be. In discovery they were emphatic about one thing above the rest ("It's just so damn COMPLICATED!"). Find what they hate most about the competitors and make it your best quality. Perplexity did this to search: after twenty years of blue links, a direct, cited answer made it feel like a different category.

Then make it last. Most feature advantages are temporary; a well-funded competitor can copy a feature in a quarter or two. Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers catalogs durable advantages, including switching costs, network effects, and brand. Brand is the trust you earn in your market, one of the few advantages a competitor can't simply outspend. Earn it in your beachhead first. Expand too quickly and you'll be a mile wide and an inch deep.

## Use it when

- Differentiation scored under 3, or Differentiation 2 (genuinely unique and valuable) or 3 (strong brand) is low.
- Customers can't say why they chose you, or deals are lost on price.
- The roadmap is a list of catch-up features.
- Before #20 Messaging Canvas, since positioning depends on this.

## Before you start

The best input is #17 Whales, Piranhas & Open Water and #19 Competitive Intelligence Map. If they're done, pull the axes, whale and piranha positions, and open water straight in and confirm them in steps 1 to 3. If not, run steps 1 to 3 quickly here and tag placements [Assumption].

Ask for discovery notes on what customers tried and what sucked most about it. Without those, the 10X factor is a guess; tag it [Assumption] and make "what's the worst part of what you use today?" the first question in the next five interviews.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 7: Two axes**
> What are the two dimensions that matter most when your customers choose, each as "this vs that"?

Push for: axes customers use, from #17 if available. Probe: "Who said that mattered?"

**Step 2 of 7: Whales and piranhas**
> Where do the big, broad players sit, and where do the cheap commodity players cluster?

Push for: named companies and workarounds on each side. Keep it brief if #17 is done.

**Step 3 of 7: Open water**
> What's the gap you can own, and who lives there?

Push for: a segment with real pain and money, not just white space.

**Step 4 of 7: Exact alternatives**
> When a customer considers you, what exactly are they comparing you to?

Push for: specific products, named workarounds, and "do nothing." This is Differentiation 4. If they only name direct competitors, ask what the last three customers used before they found you.

**Step 5 of 7: Only-ness**
> Fill in the blanks: "We're the only ___ that ___ for ___."

Push for: a real category, one capability, one segment. Test: "Could a competitor say this sentence truthfully?" If yes, tighten it.

**Step 6 of 7: 10X factor**
> What's the one thing you do dramatically better, and what are you willing to be worse at to get there?

Push for: the thing customers hate most about the alternatives, flipped into your best quality, plus the tradeoff named out loud. Probe: "Ten times better by what measure?"

**Step 7 of 7: Moat**
> If a well-funded competitor copied your best feature next quarter, why would customers still stay with you?

Push for: a power that grows with scale or time: switching costs, network effects, brand. "We'll move faster" is a head start; ask what gets harder to copy the bigger you get.

## Push back when

- **The only-ness sentence fits any competitor.** "If they could say it too, it isn't only-ness. What's true of you alone?"
- **There's no tradeoff.** "To have a 10X factor you have to give something up. What are you choosing to be worse at?"
- **The 10X factor is a feature list.** "That's a pile of features. Which one thing would make them switch?"
- **The moat is a head start.** "Anything you can build, they can copy in a quarter or two. What compounds as you grow?"
- **They want to expand before owning the beachhead.** "Earn the reputation here first, or you'll be a mile wide and an inch deep."
- **The workbook's test:** "If a customer can't name the one reason they chose you, you haven't differentiated enough."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the canvas revealed: whether the 10X factor maps to what customers hate most, whether the only-ness survives the "could a competitor say it" test, whether the moat is real.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] field with the cheapest test (for example, "ask five recent wins why they chose us and see if they name the 10X factor unprompted").

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "ask five recent customers, unprompted, the one reason they chose us, and compare it to the only-ness line."

## Done well

- The only-ness sentence is true of this company alone.
- The 10X factor traces back to a specific customer complaint about alternatives.
- A tradeoff is named and the founder accepts it.
- The moat gets stronger with scale or time.
