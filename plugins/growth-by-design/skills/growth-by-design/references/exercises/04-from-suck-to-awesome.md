## Why it matters

This is where a stack of interviews becomes one page. Move left to right: the pain they named, the awesome they pictured, and the exact words they used. The right-hand column becomes your design brief and your messaging. It's the hinge of Don't Suck, Be Awesome, the point where the "suck" half of the conversation (Chapter 4) turns into what you build (Chapter 6) and how you sell it (Chapter 9).

Each column has a job downstream. The "what sucks" column, ranked, points at the problem worth solving first: the worst pain you can credibly fix. The "awesome" column turns into design principles, the short guardrails for how the product should feel and what it should never be. Chapter 6's rule is blunt: if a feature, screen, or flow doesn't follow the principle, it sucks, and it doesn't ship. The verbatim column is your copy. Chapter 9 says don't translate their pain into polished ad-speak; if they call it "pure chaos every morning," you sell them calm mornings.

The mistake founders make is filling the awesome column themselves. They hear the pain, then write the solution in their own vocabulary, usually the features they already planned. That severs the link between what customers asked for and what gets built and sold. Every "wouldn't it be awesome if..." from discovery is a gain waiting to become a value proposition, but only if it's still in the customer's voice. The workbook's line: if the awesome column is in your words instead of theirs, you haven't finished listening.

Dollar Shave Club is the book's proof of what verbatim language can do: a launch video in the plain, slightly annoyed words a real customer would use, with no ad gloss.

## Use it when

- The founder has five or more interviews and no synthesis.
- The team can't agree which problem to solve first.
- Messaging and product direction seem to come from different places.
- They're about to write design principles or a landing page and have nothing in customers' words.

## Before you start

This table is built from interviews. Ask for their notes, transcripts, or #9 Customer Interview Tracker. With fewer than five conversations, say so: the table will be thin and the "one problem" pick is a hypothesis. Offer to run #3 DSBA Interview Guide first, or build the table now and tag every row without a quote [Assumption]. A row with no verbatim words is a question for the next interview, not a finding. Never block them.

## Run it

The table loops row by row. Aim for three to seven rows, one per distinct pain.

**Step 1 of 5: What sucks (loop per row)**
> What's one pain customers named? Give it to me the way they described it.

Push for: a single, specific pain per row, sourced to who said it. If two pains are tangled, split them. Probe: "How many people raised this one?"

**Step 2 of 5: What would be awesome (loop per row)**
> For that pain, what did they picture instead? What did they say awesome would look like?

Push for: the outcome they described, from Stage 4 of the interview. If the answer is a feature the founder plans to build, ask: "Did a customer say that, or did you?" If no customer said it, tag it [Assumption].

**Step 3 of 5: Their exact words (loop per row)**
> What's the exact phrase they used, word for word?

Push for: a verbatim quote, with emotional heat if there is any. "I freaking hate it when..." beats a paraphrase. If they don't have one, leave the cell empty and note the row needs another interview. Then return to Step 1 for the next row.

**Step 4 of 5: The one problem to solve first**
> Looking at every row, which is the worst pain you can credibly fix?

Push for: one row, chosen on two tests. It hurts the most, judged by how often it came up and how hot the language was. And the founder can credibly fix it with what they have. Probe: "Why this one over the second-worst?"

**Step 5 of 5: The verbatim phrase to build the brand around**
> Which line did customers repeat, the one that sounds like the voice already in their heads?

Push for: a phrase that came up more than once, in their words. If it only appeared once, keep it as a candidate and tag it [Assumption] until someone else says it.

## Push back when

- **The awesome column sounds like the founder.** "If the awesome column is in your words instead of theirs, you haven't finished listening. Go back to the transcripts."
- **Every row says the same thing.** "These are one pain written five ways. Merge them. What else sucks?"
- **The verbatim column is a summary.** "That's a paraphrase. What did they actually say?"
- **The one problem is the one they wanted all along.** "Would you pick it if you'd never had the idea? Show me which rows point to it."
- **They pick a pain they can't fix.** "The rule is the worst pain you can credibly fix. What can you actually deliver?"


**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences: how many rows are backed by quotes, whether the awesome column is still in the customer's voice, and whether the chosen problem is the strongest row or the founder's favorite.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] row or field with the interview question that would settle it.

**Your next move this week**: read the brand phrase and the one-problem row to a real customer and ask them to say it back in their own words.

## Done well

- Every row has a verbatim quote, or is marked as needing one.
- The awesome column reads like customers talking.
- The one problem is defensible from the rows, not from the founder's roadmap.
- The brand phrase was said by more than one person.
