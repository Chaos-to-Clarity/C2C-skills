## Why it matters

You can spend a year building your dream product and find out nobody wants it, or find out next week for the price of a few ads or cups of coffee. That's the strategy of the Solution pillar: validate at the lowest possible cost, and defer the expensive work of building and marketing until you know it's worth it. Production software is still the most expensive cost in the business and the hardest to change, so prove the idea while it's cheap to be wrong.

The book's cheapest market test is a landing page plus a few dollars in ads. Put the core message on a simple page (Leadpages, Unbounce, or Webflow), write a few copy variants to A/B test, and add an email signup with a hook like "Be the first to get access." Run Facebook, LinkedIn, or Google ads aimed at the problem. The book's benchmarks: expect click-through in the low-to-mid single digits, and landing-page-to-signup conversion that's high, often 20 percent or more. Follow up with a founder email and a short survey reusing the discovery questions. If the funnel converts and the survey says the problem is severe, you're onto something. If not, try new message variants, and if nothing moves, go back and check the problem and solution are actually valid.

For the product itself, fake it in a prototype. Keep it ugly on purpose: grey wireframes and placeholder text invite more honest feedback, because users feel the app can still change. Polish makes them push less. When it's time for something functional, build the front end first with hard-coded dummy data, and defer the back end. Then beta test by handing it over and shutting up: give a scenario and a goal, don't guide them, and treat getting lost as a design flaw. Include brand-new users, since early users have climbed the learning curve. Afterwards ask the book's six questions: how they felt, how well it solved their problem, where it needs work, how it compares to competitors, what it would take to convert, and what they'd pay.

Figma is the book's example: years in private beta, rebuilding the rendering engine and refining with designers before launching publicly. Aim hard work at a felt problem and iterate your way to the solution.

## Use it when

- The founder is about to commit real money or engineering time to an untested idea.
- Solution scored under 3, or Solution 3, 4, or 5 (validated with data) scored 1 or 2.
- Validation so far is friends saying "I'd use that."

## Before you start

Ask for the problem and job this solution addresses (#11 and #12 if done). The workbook's rule: one card per risky assumption, and run the cheapest test that can kill it first. Most founders have several risky beliefs. List them, pick the riskiest, and run a card. Then offer the next card.

Cards are designed now and finished later. If the test hasn't run, leave Result and Decision as "pending" with a date to return. If the founder already ran a test, fill the card from what happened. Anything stated without evidence gets tagged [Assumption]. Don't block them.

## Run it

Loop steps 1 to 6 once per card. Repeat for as many assumptions as the founder wants to test.

**Step 1 of 6: List the beliefs**
> What has to be true for this idea to work? List every belief you're betting on.

Push for: beliefs about demand, the problem, usability, willingness to pay, and reach. Pull from [Assumption] tags on earlier canvases.

**Step 2 of 6: Riskiest assumption**
> Which of those, if it's wrong, sinks the whole idea?

Push for: one belief, stated so it could be false. "People want this" is too soft. Ask: "Wrong about which part, for whom?"

**Step 3 of 6: Cheapest test**
> What's the cheapest, fastest test that could prove this wrong?

Push for: a landing page, storyboard, ad test, ugly prototype, concierge, or interview, before any production code. If they propose building the feature, ask: "What could you learn this week without writing a line of code?"

**Step 4 of 6: Success metric and threshold**
> What result proves it, and what number do you have to hit? Decide now, before you run it.

Push for: a metric and a number written in advance. For a landing page test, use the book's benchmarks as the starting bar. For a beta test, task completion without help. If they won't commit to a number, ask: "What result would make you stop?"

**Pause point.** Steps 5 and 6 need the test to have run. If they don't have it yet, stop here: save the canvas as a draft with the steps done so far, add the field work to Open next moves with a date, and tell them to say "continue" when they have results. Resume at the next step.

**Step 5 of 6: Result**
> What actually happened?

Push for: the raw numbers and what users did, plus answers to the six post-test questions if it was a beta. If the test hasn't run, set the date to come back.

**Step 6 of 6: Decision**
> Given the threshold you set, do you persevere, pivot, or kill?

Push for: a call that matches the threshold. If the number missed and they want to persevere, ask: "What would you have to see to call this dead?" Then: "What's the next riskiest assumption?"

## Push back when

- **The threshold gets set after the result.** "You picked the number after you saw the data. Set it first, or the test can't fail."
- **The test is the product.** "Building it is the most expensive test there is. The goal is to be wrong fast and cheap, before the money is gone."
- **Validation came from friends.** "Validating with a few friends is easy. What happens in front of the open market?"
- **The prototype is polished.** "Pretty invites polite feedback. Make it ugly until you've nailed how it works."
- **They walked the user through it.** "If you had to guide them, the test told you about you. Hand it over and shut up."
- **Weak numbers, persevere anyway.** "Try new message variants. If nothing moves, go back a step and check the problem."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the cards revealed, for example whether the riskiest belief is being tested first, whether thresholds were set in advance, and how much is still untested.

**Assumptions to test**: the beliefs from Step 1 that don't have a card yet, each with the cheapest test.

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "launch the test on Card 1 by [day] and put the review date on the calendar."

## Done well

- Each card tests one belief that could actually sink the idea.
- The threshold was written down before the test ran.
- The test costs days and dollars, not months and engineers.
- Every finished card ends in an honest persevere, pivot, or kill.
