## Why it matters

Most founders aim too wide, a product "for small businesses" or "for developers," and resonate with no one. The winners start narrow and expand only as fast as their understanding grows. Pick a beachhead: one small, homogeneous group you can address completely before you expand. Geoffrey Moore made the case in Crossing the Chasm: take one beach, win it, then roll into the adjacent ones from a position of strength. Bill Aulet builds his whole method in Disciplined Entrepreneurship around choosing a single beachhead and profiling one real customer in obsessive detail.

Choose the beachhead by four hard criteria: the pain is urgent, the people are reachable, they have money to spend, and they talk to each other so word can spread. Superhuman is the book's example. Rahul Vohra didn't open his email app to everyone who wanted faster email; he started with high-volume professionals who lived in their inbox and walked each through a personal onboarding until they were hooked.

Then size it from the bottom up, Aulet's way. Skip the "it's a fifty billion dollar market" fantasy math every investor has heard a thousand times. Count the real, reachable customers in the beachhead, multiply by what they'll actually pay, and you get a number you can defend. That's the SOM, the slice you can win now. SAM and TAM matter later, when you're raising money and expanding, so TAM goes last. The workbook's line: a $1M SOM you can actually capture beats a $1B TAM you can't.

The mistake founders make is starting from the top: a big industry number, a small percentage, and a market size nobody can act on.

## Use it when

- The founder's market size comes from an analyst report, not a count.
- They can't say how many customers they could reach in year one.
- They're torn between several segments and need to choose one.
- They're preparing for fundraising and the market slide is top-down.
- The SOM field on #7 Persona Canvas is a guess.

## Before you start

Ask which segment they're sizing. If they have several candidates, size the strongest one first, or run the four criteria on each in Step 1 and pick. One sheet, one beachhead.

Counts should come from primary research: lists they can build, communities they can see, customers they've talked to, real price conversations. If a number comes from a guess or a report, tag it [Assumption] and name the cheapest way to firm it up. Never block them.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 8: Your beachhead segment**
> What's the one narrow group you'll win first? Then check it: is the pain urgent, are they reachable, do they have money, and do they talk to each other?

Push for: a group tight enough to find by name, and an honest yes or no on each criterion. If one fails, say which and ask whether a narrower slice passes.

**Step 2 of 8: How many of them exist?**
> How many of these people or companies exist? Where does that number come from?

Push for: a count built from primary research: a list, a directory, a community's membership, a database they can pull. Probe: "Could you get me the first 50 names?"

**Step 3 of 8: How many can you reach in year one?**
> Realistically, how many of them can you reach in year one, and through what?

Push for: a number tied to real reach: channels, contacts, the founder's own capacity. If they reuse Step 2's number, ask: "Can you really reach every one of them this year?"

**Step 4 of 8: What would they pay per year?**
> What would one of them pay you per year? What tells you that?

Push for: a price grounded in conversations, current spend on workarounds, or willingness-to-pay signals from interviews. If it's a guess, tag it [Assumption] and point to #24 Pricing Test Canvas.

**Step 5 of 8: SOM (Year 1)**
> Multiply reachable customers by annual price. What's the SOM?

Push for: the math shown. Ask: "Is that a number you'd defend in front of an investor, line by line?"

**Step 6 of 8: SAM**
> Now take all segment members times the annual price. What's the SAM?

Push for: Step 2's count times Step 4's price. Keep it to this segment.

**Step 7 of 8: TAM, last**
> What's the total addressable market, the industry estimate?

Push for: a source, and a reminder that this number comes last for a reason. Don't let it change the plan.

**Step 8 of 8: Adjacent segments (from the book)**
> Once you've won this beach, which two or three neighboring segments would you roll into next?

Push for: segments that share the pain, the language, or the channel, so the win carries over. Probe: "What makes this one adjacent rather than just bigger?"

## Push back when

- **The segment is a category.** "That's a market, not a beachhead. Who inside it feels the pain most urgently and talks to each other?"
- **The math starts at TAM.** "Skip the fantasy math. Count real, reachable customers first. TAM goes last."
- **Reachable equals total.** "You can't reach everyone in year one. How many, through what?"
- **The price is a hope.** "Who told you they'd pay that? Until someone does, it's an assumption to test."
- **The SOM feels too small.** "A $1M SOM you can actually capture beats a $1B TAM you can't. Start narrow, win completely, then move."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences: whether the beachhead passes all four criteria, which number is weakest, whether the SOM is defensible line by line, and whether the adjacent segments are real neighbors.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] number with the cheapest way to firm it up, for example building the first 50 names or asking five prospects what they spend today.

**Your next move this week**: build the list behind Step 2 and contact the first ten names, which checks reachability and price at the same time.

## Done well

- The beachhead passes all four criteria, or the founder knows which one fails and why they're accepting it.
- The SOM is built from counts and prices with a stated source for each.
- TAM was filled last and didn't change the plan.
- Adjacent segments share something real with the beachhead.
