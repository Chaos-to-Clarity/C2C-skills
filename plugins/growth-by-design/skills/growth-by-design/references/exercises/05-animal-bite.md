## Why it matters

Not every problem is worth solving, and not all pain is the same. Two things matter most: how often the problem happens and how much it hurts, frequency and severity. The value of a problem is roughly frequency times severity. The Animal Bite Framework adds the shape of the pain, how it shows up over time, and that shape tells you how you have to sell and deliver the product.

The four bites, with the book's examples:

- **Mosquito.** Not dangerous, but a constant, low-grade annoyance that doesn't go away. The solution has to be very easy to adopt, and it will be super sticky. Calendly solves the annoyance of coordinating meetings: not a huge problem, but once you switch, you never go back. Sell with low cost and simple onboarding, and earn it back through retention and referrals.
- **Dog.** Sharp and intense in the moment, then it subsides. Uber: most people use it occasionally, but when you need a ride now, you need it. There's a narrow window of urgency, so catch the customer in the moment. Packaging and positioning are everything.
- **Snake.** Barely registers when it happens, but left alone it will kill you. Security apps like Crowdstrike: you can gamble you won't get hacked or audited, but eventually it's catastrophic. It's a trap, a real pain the customer can't feel yet, so your job is to educate them on the real cost before it hits.
- **Shark.** Immediate and lethal at once; the customer is already bleeding. Hit by a DDoS attack, you sign up for Cloudflare and reroute your traffic. Sell immediate relief in an emergency, at a high premium.

The best opportunities are the important problems today's tools serve poorly. Dan Olsen makes this the heart of The Lean Product Playbook: hunt for needs that are important and underserved. Pick the top two or three and start to shape a solution. The book also names four shapes most new opportunities take: simplify a complex workflow, automate a manual process, integrate across the gap between tools, or appropriate a proven model from another domain (the "Uber for X" move, used carefully, since a new paradigm doesn't automatically make things better).

Treat all pain as equal and the go-to-market drifts out of fit with the problem: a mosquito sold with heavy onboarding, a dog that can't be found in the moment, or a snake pitched as if customers already feel it.

## Use it when

- The founder has a list of problems and no way to compare them.
- Sales cycles feel wrong for the product: nobody's urgent, or onboarding is too heavy for a small pain.
- They're choosing between pricing approaches or channels and don't know what the pain demands.
- Problem 2 or Problem 5 scored under 3.

## Before you start

The ratings come from interviews, not gut. Ask for #9 Customer Interview Tracker or notes. If they have no interviews, run the exercise anyway with every rating tagged [Assumption], and turn each into an interview question about how often it happens and what it costs. Never block them.

This is the quick classification pass. #11 Problem Canvas does the full scoring and ranking; the two use the same 1 to 5 scales.

## Run it

Loop steps 1 to 4 once per problem. Aim for three to seven. Then do steps 5 and 6.

**Step 1 of 6: The problem (loop)**
> What's one problem your customers told you about, in their words?

Push for: one pain per entry, phrased as the customer experiences it, not as a missing feature.

**Step 2 of 6: Frequency and severity (loop)**
> How often does it bite them, and how badly does it hurt when it does? Rate each 1 to 5.

Push for: evidence behind each number: how many people raised it, how often it happens, what it costs in time, money, or sanity. Emotional language is the severity signal. Probe: "Who said that, and what exactly did they say?"

**Step 3 of 6: The bite (loop)**
> Which bite is it: mosquito, dog, snake, or shark?

Push for: a type that matches the pattern over time. Constant and low-grade is a mosquito. Sharp and occasional is a dog. Silent until catastrophic is a snake. Immediate and lethal is a shark. If they say shark, ask: "Is the customer bleeding right now? Would they pay a premium today?"

**Step 4 of 6: Underserved? (loop)**
> What do they use for this today, and how poorly does it serve them?

Push for: named tools and duct-taped workarounds. An important problem with a good existing fix is a weak opportunity. Then go to the next problem.

**Step 5 of 6: What the top bite means**
> For your strongest problem, what does its bite tell you about how you have to sell and deliver?

Push for: specific implications. Mosquito: dead-simple onboarding, low price, a plan for retention and referrals. Dog: be findable in the moment of urgency, sharp packaging. Snake: content that teaches the real cost. Shark: fast relief, premium pricing. Probe: "Does your current go-to-market match that?"

**Step 6 of 6: Impact shape**
> Which move are you making on that problem: simplify, automate, integrate, or appropriate?

Push for: one shape tied to a workaround they observed. If "appropriate", ask what the new model actually improves for this customer.

## Push back when

- **Everything is a shark.** "If every problem is an emergency, none of them is. Which one would they pay a premium to stop today?"
- **Ratings come from the founder's gut.** "Pull the ratings from customer interviews. Who told you it hurts this much?"
- **A snake is being sold like a shark.** "They can't feel this one yet. Your job is to teach them the real cost before it hits."
- **A mosquito comes with heavy onboarding.** "Mosquito pain won't justify effort. Make adoption effortless, or they'll keep scratching."
- **The problem is important but well served.** "Important and underserved. Which half is missing here?"

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences: which bite dominates, whether the current go-to-market fits it, how many ratings rest on evidence, and whether the top problem is underserved.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] rating with the interview question that would settle it, for example "How often did this happen last month, and what did it cost you?"

**Your next move this week**: check one part of the go-to-market against the top bite and change it, or ask three customers how often the top problem bit them last month.

## Done well

- Every rating points to something customers said or did.
- The founder can explain why the top problem is that bite, and what it changes about selling.
- At least one problem was demoted because a decent fix already exists.
