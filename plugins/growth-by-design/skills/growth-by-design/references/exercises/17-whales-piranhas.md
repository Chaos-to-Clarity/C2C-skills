## Why it matters

A product that's only a little better than the alternatives gives nobody a reason to switch. Customers already have a tool, a workaround, or a way of coping, and inertia is real. To win, you need a place in the market where you can be dramatically better for one group, and that starts with seeing the whole landscape at once.

Eric's model: most markets have a few whales, big players that eat a large share of a broad market. They aren't specialized and don't do any one thing especially well, but they have deep resources and vertical integration. Then there's a swarm of piranhas, small players in a commoditized race to the bottom. You don't want to fight in either water. You want open water: the gap between them, an underserved segment nobody serves well. Fighting the whales head on gets you crushed, and fighting the piranhas gets you scraps.

The trick is choosing the right axes. The book offers examples: Global vs Local, Simple vs Complex, Affordable vs Expensive, Manual vs Automated, Flexible vs Constrained, Economy vs Luxury, DIY vs Guided. The possibilities are endless; pick the two that matter most to your customer. The common mistake is picking axes that flatter the founder (for example, an axis built around their own technology) instead of the dimensions customers actually choose on. Perplexity is the book's picture of open water: search worked the same way for twenty years until Perplexity changed the output from a page of blue links to a direct, cited answer, and it felt like a different category to the people who tried it.

## Use it when

- The founder says "we have no real competitors" or lists only the giants.
- Differentiation scored under 3, or Differentiation 4 (name the alternatives and the one thing you do better) is a 1 or 2.
- The pitch claims to be better at everything.
- Before #18 Differentiation Canvas, which starts from these axes.

## Before you start

The map is only as good as the competitor list. Ask whether they've done #19 Competitive Intelligence Map or have discovery notes on what customers tried before. Real competitors include workarounds (spreadsheets, an intern, doing nothing). If the list comes from the founder's head, proceed and tag placements [Assumption]; every placement becomes a question for the next five interviews ("Where would you put X on this?").

If the session can render visuals, offer to draw the grid once competitors are placed. Otherwise produce the text grid in the Output.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 7: The customer's choice**
> When your customer picks between options, what two things decide it for them?

Push for: decision criteria in customer language, ideally quoted from interviews. Offer the book's list of axes if they stall. Probe: "Which customer told you that mattered?"

**Step 2 of 7: Lock the axes**
> Of those, which two make the competitors spread out the most? Name each axis as "this vs that."

Push for: two independent dimensions. If every competitor lands in the same spot, the axis isn't doing work. If the axes are basically the same thing (cheap vs expensive and economy vs luxury), swap one.

**Step 3 of 7: Place the whales**
> Who are the big, broad, well-resourced players, and where does each one sit on the grid?

Push for: named companies placed by how customers experience them, not by their marketing. Ask what segment each whale is really going after.

**Step 4 of 7: Place the piranhas**
> Who are the small, cheap, commodity players, and where do they cluster?

Push for: the swarm, including DIY tools and workarounds. Ask: "What are they racing to the bottom on?"

**Step 5 of 7: Find the open water**
> Where on this grid is there a blank spot that a real group of customers would want to stand in?

Push for: a gap with people in it. An empty quadrant can mean nobody wants it. Probe: "Name three customers who live in that spot today and what they use instead."

**Step 6 of 7: Place yourself**
> Where do you sit right now, and where do you need to sit to own that gap?

Push for: an honest current position. If they're already in the whale or piranha zone, name the move required.

**Step 7 of 7: The switching difference**
> What's the one difference that would make customers in that gap switch to you?

Push for: one thing, tied to what customers said sucks most about the alternatives. "Better UX" isn't enough; ask "Better how, measured how, and would they leave their current tool for it?"

## Push back when

- **"We have no competitors."** "Then what are your customers doing today? That's your competitor, even if it's a spreadsheet."
- **The axes flatter the founder.** "Would a customer use those words when choosing? If not, pick the axes they'd pick."
- **They place themselves alone in a great quadrant with no evidence.** "Who besides you says you belong there? Tag it [Assumption] until a customer does."
- **The open water is empty for a reason.** "Is this a gap or a desert? Who's standing there with money and pain?"
- **They want to be better at everything.** "No one can be the best at everything. Pick one difference and own it."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the map revealed, for example whether the axes came from customers, whether the gap holds real buyers, and whether the founder is currently swimming with whales or piranhas.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] placement or claim with the interview question that settles it (for example, "Show three customers the grid and ask where they'd put us").

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "show this grid to five customers in the gap and ask what would make them switch."

## Done well

- Both axes are dimensions customers use to choose, with a quote behind each.
- Workarounds and DIY options appear on the map.
- The open water has named people in it, not just white space.
- The switching difference is one thing, specific enough to measure.

## Next

- Run #18 Differentiation Canvas to turn the gap into only-ness, a 10X factor, and a moat. If the competitor list felt thin, run #19 Competitive Intelligence Map first.
