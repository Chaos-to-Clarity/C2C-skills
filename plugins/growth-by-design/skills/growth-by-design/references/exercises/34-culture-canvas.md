## Why it matters

Human-Centered Design builds products customers love, but only if the whole company revolves around delivering customer value. Once the team grows past the founders, keeping that focus takes deliberate effort. The workbook frames it as designing culture on purpose, the way you'd design a product.

The book's example is Netflix. It wrote down the culture it actually wanted, freedom paired with responsibility, then hired, promoted, and let people challenge it openly. The culture deck became one of the most shared documents in Silicon Valley because it treated culture as something you design. Decide what you value, put it in writing, and let it shape who you hire and how you lead.

The chapter gives six moves. **Hire for mission:** you can teach anyone to code, but you can't teach someone to care. A candidate for a wellness app who doesn't care about wellness won't be inspired, and won't stay when it gets hard. **Inspire, don't just incentivize:** the assembly-line model grades engineers on velocity; a human-centered culture grades them on love letters and five-star reviews. Cash buys output; stories about changed lives inspire work that matters. **You're the machine, not the cog:** connect daily work to the purpose so people think in solutions, experiences, and people instead of tasks, features, and users. **A face to build for:** share photos, video, and testimonials, and get engineers in the room with customers at events or small focus groups. **Make it a ritual:** a monthly event that reads the kudos, owns the failures, and awards customer champions. **Live like your customers:** build a mood board of their world (products, music, clothes, language, food, places). Eric took it further: his team switched from Spotify to Apple Music because their customers used it, and the interface began to take on the same feel. The engineers knew, without being told, exactly what the color blue meant.

## Use it when

- The team has grown past the founders and decisions are drifting from the customer.
- Engineers are measured on velocity and have never met a customer.
- Product leaders have to spell out every detail because the team doesn't know the customer's world.
- Hiring screens for skill and nothing else.
- #29 Idea Meritocracy Audit scored Tightly Integrated or Customer-Centered low.

## Before you start

Ask how big the team is and how often anyone outside the founders talks to a customer. Ask whether values are written down anywhere and whether the team could repeat them. If they don't know, that's the first finding; the workbook's line is that a team that can't say what the company stands for has a culture forming by accident.

A grounded #7 Persona Canvas or #8 Empathy Map makes Steps 4 and 6 far stronger. Without one, keep going and tag those fields [Assumption].

Any field describing something that doesn't happen yet is a plan, not a practice. Tag it [Assumption] and give it a start date.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 6: Stated values**
> What does your organization actually value? Write it as if you had to put it on the wall tomorrow.

Push for: a few values specific enough to settle a real decision. Probe: "When did one of these cost you something, like a hire you passed on or a deal you walked from?" Values that never cost anything are slogans.

**Step 2 of 6: Hire for mission**
> What's the one belief every new hire has to share, even if they need to learn the skills?

Push for: a belief about the customer or the problem, and how they test for it in an interview. Probe: "What question tells you a candidate cares?"

**Step 3 of 6: Inspire, not just incentivize**
> How do you reward customer impact today, not just output?

Push for: what actually gets celebrated or measured. If the answer is velocity or tickets closed, ask: "What would it look like to track love letters and five-star reviews instead?"

**Step 4 of 6: A face to build for**
> How do you put real customers in front of your team, especially your engineers?

Push for: specific, recurring mechanisms: recordings, photos and testimonials in the workspace, engineers on calls, client events, small focus groups. Probe: "When did an engineer last hear a customer in their own voice?"

**Step 5 of 6: The monthly ritual**
> How do you celebrate the wins and own the failures, every month?

Push for: a dated, recurring event that includes both. If only wins get shared, ask where the failures go. Suggest an award for whoever championed a customer win.

**Step 6 of 6: Live like your customers**
> How do you immerse the team in your customers' world? What would go on their mood board?

Push for: concrete references (the products they use, their music, clothes, language, food, the places they live and work). Ask what the team would change in its own environment to match, the way Eric's team switched music services.

## Push back when

- **Values are generic.** "Every company says integrity. What would make someone wrong for your company even if they're great at the job?"
- **Hiring is skill-first.** "You can teach anyone to code. You can't teach them to care about this mission. How do you find out?"
- **Incentives reward speed alone.** "Cash gets you more output. Stories about whose life changed get you work that matters. Which is on your team's scorecard?"
- **Engineers never meet customers.** "Without a face to build for, someone has to spell out every hex value. How do they get in the room?"
- **The ritual is wins only.** "Owning the failures is half the ritual. Where did you let a customer down this month?"
- **Nobody can say what the company stands for.** "Then your culture is forming by accident instead of by design. Let's write it down."


**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on how close the team sits to the customer, which field is mostly plan rather than practice, and where the founder is still the only bridge to customers.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] field, with the simplest check (ask three team members to state the values, count engineer customer contacts this quarter).

**Your next move this week**: one action, usually "put the first monthly ritual on the calendar and have an engineer present one customer story."

## Done well

- Values are written, specific, and have cost the company something at least once.
- Every engineer has heard a real customer in the last month, or there's a dated plan.
- The ritual includes failures, not only wins.
- The mood board exists and someone outside the founders can describe the customer's world.
