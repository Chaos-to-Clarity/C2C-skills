## Why it matters

Your real competitors are the products your customers consider when they consider you. That list is rarely the one in the pitch deck. It includes the whale everyone knows, the cheap tools, and the workarounds: a spreadsheet, an agency, an intern, or living with the pain. Inertia is a competitor too. A product that's only slightly better than what customers already have gives them no reason to switch.

The point of the map is to find what to beat. In discovery, customers tell you what they tried and where it let them down, and they're usually most emphatic about one thing. Chapter 8's instruction: find the thing they hate most about the competitors and make that your best quality. This map collects that evidence competitor by competitor, then rolls it up into three insights: what none of them do well, what they all do that you can leapfrog, and which segment they've left underserved. Those insights are the raw material for open water in #17 and only-ness in #18.

The column that matters most is win/loss. Differentiation 5 asks whether customers name your specific difference as the deciding factor in won and lost deals. If you don't track why you win and lose against each competitor, you're guessing about your own differentiation. Markets move, so update this every quarter.

## Use it when

- The founder can't list what customers used before them.
- Differentiation 4 or 5 scored 1 or 2.
- Deals are lost and nobody knows why, or the team argues about who the real competitor is.
- Quarterly, as a refresh, and before #17 or #18.

## Before you start

Ask for three sources: discovery notes (what customers tried), win/loss notes from recent deals, and public material (competitor sites, reviews). The "How customers describe them" column must come from customers, not competitor marketing. If they have few conversations, fill what they can, tag the rest [Assumption], and turn each blank into an interview question: "What else did you look at, and what did you think of it?"

This is a table tool. Loop row by row, one competitor at a time, then do the summary fields.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 4: Build the list**
> What did your last five customers use, or seriously consider, before choosing you?

Push for: five to eight entries, including at least one workaround and "do nothing." Probe if they list only big names: "What did the customer who almost didn't buy use instead?"

**Step 2 of 4: Fill each row** (repeat per competitor)

Ask one at a time, acknowledging each answer:

> Which customer segment does [competitor] really serve?

> What's their strongest claim, the thing they'd say about themselves?

> What's their biggest weakness, in your customers' experience?

> How do customers describe them, in customers' own words?

> What's your win/loss record against them, and why?

Push for: a quote in the "how customers describe them" column. For weakness, ask "Who complained about that, and what did they say?" For win/loss, push for a count and the reason given, even if the count is small. "We usually win" without a reason gets tagged [Assumption].

**Step 3 of 4: Roll up the patterns**
> Looking across every row, what's the one thing none of them do well?

Then:
> What's the one thing they all do that you could leapfrog?

Push for: a pattern that shows up in the weakness and description columns, not the founder's opinion. For the leapfrog, ask: "Is that a shared habit customers hate, like a page of blue links was for search?" (The book's Perplexity example.)

**Step 4 of 4: The underserved segment**
> Which segment have they all left underserved?

Push for: a named group with real pain. Compare it to the founder's beachhead. If they match, that's a strong signal. If not, flag it.

## Push back when

- **No workarounds on the list.** "Your real competitors are what customers consider when they consider you. What did they do before any tool?"
- **Descriptions are copied from competitor websites.** "That's how they describe themselves. How do your customers describe them?"
- **Win/loss is blank.** "If you don't know why you win and lose, you don't know your differentiation. Start tracking it on the next deal."
- **Every weakness is "worse UX."** "Worse how, for whom, and who said it?"
- **The map is a year old.** "Markets move. This gets updated every quarter."

## Output

```
GROWTH BY DESIGN · Competitive Intelligence Map
[Company] · [Date] · Next refresh: [date + 90 days]

| Competitor | Customer segment they serve | Their strongest claim | Their biggest weakness | How customers describe them | Win/loss vs. them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [name] | [segment] | [claim] | [weakness] [E/A] | "[quote]" [E/A] | [W-L, reason] [E/A] |

The one thing none of them do well: [pattern]  [Evidence/Assumption]
The one thing they all do that we can leapfrog: [shared habit]  [Evidence/Assumption]
The segment they've left underserved: [segment]  [Evidence/Assumption]
```

[E/A] means tag the cell [Evidence] or [Assumption].

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the map revealed, for example whether workarounds are the biggest competitor, whether the underserved segment matches the beachhead, and how much of the win/loss column is real.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] cell with the cheapest test (usually one interview question or a win/loss call).

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "call two lost deals and ask what the other option did better."

## Done well

- At least one workaround or "do nothing" row.
- The description column holds customer quotes.
- Win/loss has counts and reasons for the top competitors.
- The three summary fields are patterns visible in the rows.
- A refresh date is set.
