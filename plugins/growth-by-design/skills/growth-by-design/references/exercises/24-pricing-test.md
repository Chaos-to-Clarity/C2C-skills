## Why it matters

Don't guess what people will pay. Run a test. Pricing is a fuzzy, flexible thing that needs experiments and feedback loops like product and marketing. Madhavan Ramanujam's Monetizing Innovation argues willingness to pay should shape the product from the start, so talk about price during discovery.

The workbook offers two methods, and the book adds a third. The Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter asks 10 or more target customers four questions (too cheap, good value, expensive but worth it, too expensive) to find an acceptable range and an optimal point. The anchor test shows three prices (low, mid, high) to 10 or more customers and tracks which tier they pick. The Magic Wand test, Eric's favorite, asks what they'd pay for a wand that immediately and perfectly solves the problem (the upper bound), then, once they've tried the product, how close it is to the wand on a 1 to 10 scale. A 5 out of 10 means you can charge half the wand price, and the price can rise as the score does.

The mistakes: asking friends, asking too few people, or asking "would you pay $X?" and taking a polite yes as data. Or running the test and never writing down what it means, which is why the book's version adds an Implication field.

## Use it when

- Price was set by guess, gut, or a competitor's page.
- Model 4 (tested willingness to pay) scored 1 or 2.
- #23 Model Canvas tagged price [Assumption].
- Before launching paid tiers or changing prices.

## Before you start

Confirm the segment from #7 Persona Canvas and test with target customers only. Confirm the value metric and tiers from #23 Model Canvas so customers react to a real offer. If either is unclear, run the test anyway and tag the result [Assumption].

This module has two phases. Phase one scripts the conversation. Phase two analyzes the results the founder brings back. If they arrive with data, skip to Step 5.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 8: Pick the method**
> Do you want a price range (Van Westendorp), a tier preference (anchor test), or an upper bound tied to how well the product solves it (Magic Wand)?

Push for: one method matched to stage. Early discovery: Magic Wand. A product and a rough range: Van Westendorp. Tiers ready: anchor test.

**Step 2 of 8: Who you'll ask**
> Which 10 or more target customers will you run this with, and by when?

Push for: names or a clear source, all matching the persona. Fewer than ten, tag the result [Assumption].

**Step 3 of 8: Set up the value**
> In one or two sentences, what will you show or describe before you ask about price?

Push for: the problem and outcome in the customer's words (from #20 Messaging Canvas), plus the value metric.

**Step 4 of 8: Script the questions**
Draft the script for the chosen method, then ask:
> Read this out loud. Where would a customer get confused or just be polite?

Van Westendorp, in this order: At what price would this feel so cheap it seems low quality? At what price would this feel like good value? At what price would this start to feel expensive, but still worth it? At what price would this feel too expensive to consider?
Anchor test: show low, mid, and high tiers with prices and ask which they'd choose and why.
Magic Wand: "If I could give you a magic wand that would immediately and perfectly solve this problem, how much would you pay for it?" Later, after use: "On a scale from 1 to 10, how close is this product to the magic wand?"

Push for: neutral wording with no hinted answer. Add one follow-up: "Why that number?"

**Pause point.** Steps 5 to 8 need real answers from 10 or more customers. If they don't have it yet, stop here: save the canvas as a draft with the steps done so far, add the field work to Open next moves with a date, and tell them to say "continue" when they have results. Resume at the next step.

**Step 5 of 8: Bring back the raw answers**
> Paste every answer you got, one row per customer.

Push for: raw numbers, not averages. Note who was off-segment.

**Step 6 of 8: Analyze**
Compute and present the result, then ask:
> Does this match what you heard in the room?

Van Westendorp: sort each question's answers and chart the share of respondents at each price. The acceptable range runs from where "too cheap" crosses the share who would not call it good value, up to where "too expensive" crosses the share who would not call it expensive. The optimal point is where "too cheap" and "too expensive" cross. With 10 to 20 people, treat it as a range, not precision.
Anchor test: percentage selecting each tier.
Magic Wand: median wand price, median closeness score, and wand price times score over 10.

**Step 7 of 8: Choose the price**
> What price will you set, and why?

Push for: a price inside the tested range, tied to the data, cross-checked against 10 percent of the problem's value from #23.

**Step 8 of 8: Implication**
> What does this result change in your model, packaging, or product?

Push for: a decision. Examples: raise the mid tier, change the value metric, or improve the product before raising the price because the wand score is low.

## Push back when

- **They tested with friends.** "Friends are polite. Who in your target segment will tell you the truth?"
- **Fewer than ten responses.** "That's a signal, not a range. Who else can you ask this week?"
- **"Would you pay $X?" got a yes.** "A polite yes isn't willingness to pay. Ask the open questions instead."
- **They pick a price outside the range.** "The data says otherwise. What do you know that your customers don't?"
- **The workbook's rule:** "Don't guess what people will pay. Run a test."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the test revealed: whether the current price is inside the range, whether off-segment answers skewed it, what the wand score says about the product.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] field with the cheapest follow-up (more respondents, a live price on a landing page, or a rerun after the next product release).

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "book the remaining pricing conversations" or "update the price in #23 and announce it to new customers."

## Done well

- Ten or more target customers answered, none of them friends.
- Raw answers are recorded, not just the conclusion.
- The chosen price sits inside the tested range with a stated reason.
- The Implication names a concrete change.
