## Why it matters

You can nail every other pillar and still go broke if the model doesn't work. Price too high and you scare customers away; too low and you leave money on the table. Package it wrong and they won't buy even if they need it. (Eric's line: Costco, I don't need a 10lb tub of mayo.)

Price on value, not cost. A report your software generates in a tenth of a second might save a customer two days of work, and that saved time is what you're selling. Madhavan Ramanujam (Monetizing Innovation) says willingness to pay should shape the product from the start. Eric's favorite tool is the Magic Wand test: ask what they'd pay for a wand that perfectly solves the problem, then how close your product is to it on a 1 to 10 scale, and charge that fraction. Or charge 10 percent of the problem's value and give them a 10x ROI. Point your incentives the same way as theirs. Ramp gives its card and spend software away and earns on interchange, so it wins only when it helps customers spend less.

Packaging is how the right customer says yes quickly. Choose a value metric, the unit that rises as the customer gets more value (seats, transactions, contacts, gigabytes). Patrick Campbell's work at ProfitWell shows the right value metric does more for growth than the price number itself. Tier it good-better-best so segments self-select. Above all, cut friction to the first purchase; every extra approval, stakeholder, or sales call is a chance to lose the sale.

Know the numbers cold. David Skok's "SaaS Metrics 2.0" gives the benchmarks: LTV to CAC around three to one, CAC payback under roughly twelve months, with gross margin underneath. The Rule of 40, which Brad Feld helped popularize, says growth rate plus profit margin should clear forty percent. The key warning: a weak model can look exactly like weak product-market fit, with churn and stalled growth, when the real culprit is misaligned price or margin.

## Use it when

- Price was set by copying a competitor or adding margin to cost.
- Model scored under 3, or Model 5 (unit economics) is a 1 or 2.
- Churn or stalled growth is blamed on PMF, but nobody has checked price and margin.
- Before fundraising, or before scaling a channel from #22.

## Before you start

Gather what exists: current price and packaging, CAC by channel from #22 Channel Testing Log, revenue per customer, churn, gross margin, growth rate. Pull the value of the problem from #11 Problem Canvas or #15 Outcome Canvas. Pre-revenue founders estimate and tag [Assumption]; each becomes a test.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 7: Value of the problem**
> What is solving this problem worth to one customer per year, in time or money?

Push for: a number built from the customer's numbers (hours saved times rate, revenue gained, cost avoided). Probe: "Which customer gave you that figure?" Then apply the 10 percent rule as a first price ceiling to compare against.

**Step 2 of 7: Value metric**
> What unit do you charge for, and does it rise as the customer gets more value?

Push for: a metric tied to the outcome, not to your cost. If they charge flat, ask: "When a customer gets ten times the value, do you earn more?"

**Step 3 of 7: Packaging and tiers**
> What are your good, better, and best tiers, and which segment is each one for?

Push for: tiers mapped to real segments from #7, each paying in proportion to value. If every tier is the same buyer, ask who the entry plan and top plan are for.

**Step 4 of 7: Price and the test**
> What's your price today, and how will you test willingness to pay with real customers?

Push for: a named test: the Magic Wand test, Van Westendorp, or an anchor test (#24). Ask: "What has a customer actually paid, or said they'd pay?"

**Step 5 of 7: Unit economics**
> What are your CAC, LTV, LTV:CAC, payback period, and gross margin?

Collect them one at a time. Push for: real numbers with sources, then compare to Skok's benchmarks (about 3:1 and under roughly twelve months). If CAC is unknown, send them to #22. If LTV is a guess, show how churn drives it.

**Step 6 of 7: Rule of 40**
> What's your growth rate plus your profit margin?

Push for: both numbers and the sum against forty percent. For very early companies, record it as a future gut check.

**Step 7 of 7: What has to be true**
> For this model to work, what has to be true that you haven't proven yet?

Push for: specific, testable conditions (customers accept the value metric, CAC stays under a number, the first purchase needs no committee). Also ask: "How many steps stand between a customer saying yes and paying you?"

## Push back when

- **Price is cost plus margin.** "It doesn't matter what it costs you to make, only what they get from it. What's it worth to them?"
- **Churn is blamed on product.** "A weak model can look exactly like weak PMF. Have you ruled out price and margin?"
- **The value metric doesn't scale with value.** "When they win more, do you earn more? If not, the incentives don't line up."
- **Unit economics are blank.** "Know these cold. What's the first one you can measure this week?"
- **The first purchase needs three approvals.** "Every extra step is a chance to lose the sale. Which one can you remove?"
- **The workbook's rule:** "Test willingness to pay with real prices before you bet the business on a guess."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the canvas revealed: whether price tracks value or cost, whether the economics meet the benchmarks, whether apparent PMF problems are model problems.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] field with the cheapest test (often #24 Pricing Test Canvas for price, #22 for CAC).

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "run the Magic Wand question in your next five customer calls."

## Done well

- Price is anchored to a calculated customer value.
- The value metric grows as the customer's value grows.
- Every unit economics number has a source, or a plan to measure it.
- The founder has checked whether a model problem is posing as a PMF problem.
