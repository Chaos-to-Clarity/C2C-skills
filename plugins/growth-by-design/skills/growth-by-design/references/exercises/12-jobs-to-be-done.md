## Why it matters

Customers don't buy products. They hire them to do a job. Clayton Christensen framed this as Jobs To Be Done: customers "hire" a product to make progress in their lives, and they fire it the moment it fails or something does the job better. The job is the outcome. The feature is just the thing you build to deliver it. Design for the job and the features follow. The book's line: nobody wants a project management tool, they want to ship on time without things slipping through the cracks. Nobody wants a fitness app, they want to feel strong and look good at the beach.

The canvas sits at the end of the Problem chapter because it turns the top-ranked problem into a job you can design against. Every job has three dimensions: functional (the practical task), emotional (how they want to feel), and social (how they want to be seen). Founders map the functional one and skip the other two, then wonder why a product that technically works doesn't get adopted.

Chapter 7 takes the job further. Tony Ulwick turned it into a discipline he calls Outcome-Driven Innovation: write each desired outcome as a measurable statement with a direction of improvement, a metric, and the thing being improved. "Minimize the time it takes to reconcile a month's expenses." "Reduce the likelihood of missing a deadline." Stated that way, success is testable. That's the form for the Success criteria field here, and it carries straight into #15 The Outcome Canvas. The workbook's reading list names Ulwick's Jobs to Be Done as the framework behind this canvas.

The mistake: writing the job in terms of your product ("use our app to track tasks"). A job exists whether or not you do. If the job statement only makes sense with your product in it, it's a feature description.

## Use it when

- The founder describes what the product does, but not what customers are trying to get done.
- Customers say they like the product but don't switch from what they use now.
- Outcomes 1 (we understand the goals customers are trying to reach) scored under 3.
- Before scoping an MVP, so the Yellow Brick Road points at the right goal.

## Before you start

This works best on one problem that already ranked high in #11 The Problem Canvas, backed by interview notes. Ask which problem and which persona this canvas is for. One canvas, one job, one persona.

If they haven't ranked problems, ask them to name the one their customers complain about most and tag the canvas as provisional. Any field they can't fill from what customers said or did gets tagged [Assumption] and becomes an interview goal. Don't block them.

## Run it

**Step 1 of 9: The core job**
> What are they trying to accomplish? Say it as a verb and an object, with no mention of your product.

Push for: "ship releases on time," "close the books each month." If the answer names a tool or feature, ask: "If your product didn't exist, what would they still be trying to get done?"

**Step 2 of 9: The trigger**
> What happens that makes this job urgent today?

Push for: a specific moment or event (a deadline, a customer complaint, a month-end close). If vague, ask: "Walk me through the last time a customer felt this. What set it off?"

**Step 3 of 9: Functional dimension**
> What's the practical task they need to complete?

Push for: concrete steps, inputs, and constraints. Keep it about the task, not your solution.

**Step 4 of 9: Emotional dimension**
> How do they want to feel while doing it, and after?

Push for: feelings in the customer's words, ideally verbatim ("in control," "not panicking at 11pm"). Ask: "Who said that?"

**Step 5 of 9: Social dimension**
> How do they want to be seen by others as a result?

Push for: the boss, the team, the client, the board. In B2B this is often the real buying trigger. If they say "it doesn't apply," ask: "Who looks good or bad when this job goes wrong?"

**Step 6 of 9: Current hire**
> What product or workaround do they use now to get this job done?

Push for: the real hire, including spreadsheets, email, an assistant, or doing nothing. Pull from "What they use today" in #11 if it exists.

**Step 7 of 9: Why it's a poor hire**
> Where does that current solution let them down?

Push for: specific failures across all three dimensions. Keep what they love about it, fix what fails them.

**Step 8 of 9: Success criteria**
> How do they know the job is done well? Say it as direction, metric, and thing being improved.

Push for: Ulwick's form ("minimize the time it takes to..."). If they say "it works," ask: "What would they measure to know it worked better than before?"

**Step 9 of 9: The job statement**
> Put it together: When [trigger], I want to [job], so I can [outcome].

Push for: one sentence a customer would recognize as their own. Read it back and ask: "Would your customer say yes, that's me?"

## Push back when

- **The job contains the product.** "A job exists without you. Take your product out of the sentence. What's left?"
- **Emotional and social are blank.** "People hire products to feel and look a certain way, too. What did they say about how this feels?"
- **The current hire is 'nothing.'** "Everyone gets the job done somehow, even badly. What are they doing today?"
- **Success criteria can't be measured.** "If you can't tell whether the job got done better, you can't tell whether you delivered."
- **The solution doesn't trace to the statement.** The workbook's rule: the job statement is your product brief. "If your solution doesn't map directly to it, you're building the wrong thing."

## Output

```
GROWTH BY DESIGN · Jobs-to-Be-Done Canvas
[Company] · [Persona] · [Date]

The core job (verb + object): [ ]  [Evidence/Assumption]
The trigger: [ ]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Functional dimension: [ ]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Emotional dimension: [their words]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Social dimension: [ ]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Current hire: [product or workaround]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Why it's a poor hire: [ ]  [Evidence/Assumption]
Success criteria: [direction + metric + thing improved]  [Evidence/Assumption]

Job statement:
"When [trigger], I want to [job], so I can [outcome]."
```

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the canvas revealed, for example whether the job is independent of the product, which dimension is weakest, and whether the current hire is really failing.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] field with the interview question that would settle it.

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "read the job statement to three customers and write down exactly how they correct it."

## Done well

- The core job and job statement make sense with the product removed.
- The trigger names a real moment a customer described.
- Success criteria are measurable in Ulwick's form.
- The founder can trace each core feature back to the job statement.
