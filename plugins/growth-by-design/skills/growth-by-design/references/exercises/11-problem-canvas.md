## Why it matters

The most expensive product you can build is one nobody needs. The job is to find a problem worth solving: urgent, frequent, and painful enough that people will pay to make it stop. The book's key insight: there is no shortcut for thorough research and problem validation. Confirm the need is acute before building anything.

The raw material comes from watching and listening. Shadow customers at work, capture every product they use, and hunt for the "duct-taped" solutions where they bend a tool to a purpose it wasn't built for or wire several products together to get one job done. Those workarounds are where the best opportunities hide. Most opportunities take one of four shapes: simplify, automate, integrate, or appropriate a proven model from another domain. The book warns about that last one: not every problem is improved by AI, blockchain, or smart-whatever.

Not all bites hurt the same. Two things matter most: how often the problem happens (frequency) and how much it hurts (severity). The value of a problem is roughly frequency times severity, and the strongest candidates are the important problems today's tools serve poorly. Dan Olsen makes this the heart of The Lean Product Playbook: hunt for needs that are important and underserved. The bite type tells you how to sell. Calendly is the book's mosquito: a constant, low-grade annoyance that, once solved, nobody wants to go back to.

The founder mistake: scoring problems from their own gut, then falling in love with the one they already wanted to build. The canvas forces a side-by-side comparison so the strongest opportunity rises on evidence.

## Use it when

- The founder has a solution and can't say which problem it ranks first against.
- Discovery interviews surfaced lots of pain but nobody has compared it.
- Problem scored under 3, or Problem 5 (we can quantify the cost) scored 1 or 2.

## Before you start

Ask how many target customers they've interviewed or shadowed in the last 90 days and whether they have notes. The workbook's rule: pull the ratings from customer interviews, not your gut. If they have notes from #9 Customer Interview Tracker, use those. If they've had fewer than five conversations, say so: most scores will be guesses. Fill the canvas anyway and tag every unsupported rating [Assumption], so it becomes their interview plan. Offer #3 DSBA Interview Guide to collect the missing evidence.

Agree on what 1 and 5 mean for frequency and severity before scoring (for example, "daily" vs "once a quarter"), and keep the anchors the same for every row.

## Run it

Loop steps 1 to 5 once per candidate problem. Aim for three to seven candidates. Then do steps 6 and 7.

**Step 1 of 7: Problem**
> What's a problem your customers told you about? Say it the way they said it.

Push for: a pain, in the customer's words, with emotional language if they used it. If the founder states a missing feature ("they need a dashboard"), ask: "What were they trying to do when that hurt?"

**Step 2 of 7: Frequency (1 to 5)**
> How often does this happen to them, and how many of the people you talked to brought it up?

Push for: a count from interviews ("six of nine mentioned it, most weekly"). If vague, ask: "Which customer, and what did they say?"

**Step 3 of 7: Severity (1 to 5)**
> When it happens, how much does it cost them in time, money, or sanity?

Push for: a number from real customers, and the heat in their words. "I freaking hate it when..." is a far bigger problem than "it's kind of annoying." If no cost was captured, tag [Assumption] and make it the next interview question.

**Step 4 of 7: Bite**
> Is this a mosquito, a dog, a snake, or a shark?

Push for: a type that matches the scores, and what it implies. Mosquitoes (constant, low-grade) need dead-simple onboarding. Dogs (sharp, occasional) need you there in the moment. Snakes (silent, lethal later) need education. Sharks (immediate, severe) command a premium. Use #5 The Animal Bite Framework to plot them.

**Step 5 of 7: What they use today**
> What do they use to deal with this right now, and where does it let them down?

Push for: named tools, spreadsheets, people, or duct-taped workarounds. "Nothing" usually means they haven't looked hard enough, or the problem isn't painful. Ask: "If it hurts that much, why haven't they tried anything?"

Then calculate Score = Frequency x Severity (max 25) and move to the next problem.

**Step 6 of 7: Rank**
> Looking at the scores, which two or three problems rise to the top, and does that surprise you?

Push for: the top two or three, by score and by how poorly today's tools serve them. If the founder's favorite isn't on top, say so and ask what evidence would change the ranking.

**Step 7 of 7: Impact shape**
> For your top problem, which move are you making: simplify, automate, integrate, or appropriate?

Push for: one shape and the workaround it replaces. If they say "appropriate" or "AI", ask: "What does this actually make better for them?"

## Push back when

- **Scores came from the founder's head.** "Pull the ratings from customer interviews, not your gut. Who gave you that 5?"
- **Every problem scores high.** "If everything is a 5, nothing is. Which one would they fix tomorrow if they could fix only one?"
- **It's a snake bite with low felt severity.** "The pain is real, but they can't feel it yet. Are you ready to educate them on the cost before it hits?"
- **The problem is a feature request.** "That's a solution. What problem does it fix for them?"
- **Only one candidate.** "You can't rank one thing. What else came up in the interviews?"

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the ranking revealed, for example whether the founder's favorite problem won, how much of the scoring is assumption, and what the bite type means for selling.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] score with the interview question that would settle it.

**Your next move this week**: one concrete action, usually "shadow two customers on the top problem and capture what it costs them."

## Done well

- Every frequency and severity score traces to a named customer or a count from interviews.
- The top problem has a cost in time or money from real customers.
- "What they use today" names real tools and workarounds, including duct-taped ones.
