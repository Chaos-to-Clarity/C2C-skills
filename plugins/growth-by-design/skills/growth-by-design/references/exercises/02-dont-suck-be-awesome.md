## Why it matters

Discovery feels like a maze of frameworks and data. Strip it down and it's a single human conversation. Don't Suck, Be Awesome is the method Eric uses to run it, and it's the throughline of the whole book: you meet your customer, find what sucks, picture what's awesome and build it, then sell it back to them in their own words. Every pillar after this is that same conversation with a sharper tool at each step.

The method has six steps. Know your customer as a person before you ask about problems. Ask what sucks with open, emotional questions, and map the whole landscape of frustration. Find what sucks most by stack-ranking the pain and targeting the worst problem you can solve. Picture what's awesome with one question: "Instead of [the sucky thing], wouldn't it be awesome if [the awesome thing]?" Build exactly that, turning the answers into design principles, features, and success criteria. Sell it in their words, so their verbatim language becomes your brand voice and value prop. That flip question defines what the product has to do, how it has to feel, and how you'll sell it, all before you write a line of code. You design product-market fit in the conversation instead of chasing it after launch.

The halves show up across the book. Chapter 4 is the "suck" half: work from broad to specific, and treat the heat in their words as your severity signal ("I freaking hate it when..." is a far bigger problem than "it's kind of annoying"). Chapter 6 turns the "awesome" half into design principles in two columns, what it should be and what it should not be; if a feature doesn't follow them, it sucks and doesn't ship. Chapter 9 sells it back: if they say "stuff slips through the cracks," your headline says stuff slips through the cracks. The book's example is Dollar Shave Club, whose launch video worked because it sounded like a fed-up customer talking, not a company.

The common mistake is skipping straight to the pitch, or translating the customer's pain into the founder's own polished words. Both throw away the raw material.

## Use it when

- The founder is about to start customer discovery and doesn't know how to open the conversation.
- They have a product idea but can't say, in a customer's words, what it replaces.
- Interviews so far have turned into demos or feature wish lists.
- Messaging sounds like the founder, and nobody repeats it back.
- They want to understand the whole method before running #3 DSBA Interview Guide.

## Before you start

Ask who they plan to talk to, and whether they've already had any of these conversations. If they have notes, use real answers at every step and tag them [Evidence]. If they haven't talked to anyone yet, this exercise becomes a plan: each step produces their best guess, tagged [Assumption], and the guesses become what the conversation has to confirm or kill. Don't block them. One conversation this week beats a perfect plan.

Keep it to one person or one tight segment. If they serve several, pick the one closest to the beachhead (#10 Beachhead Market Sizing).

## Run it

**Step 1 of 6: Know your customer**
> Who's the one person you're going to talk to, and what do you need to understand about their week before you ask about any problem?

Push for: a named person (or a precise role) and two or three things about their world: role, top priority, what success means to them. If they jump to problems, ask: "What would make this person trust you enough to tell you the truth?"

**Step 2 of 6: Ask what sucks**
> What's the domain you're exploring, and what's your wide-open opening question about it?

Push for: an emotional, open question about the domain, never about their product. "What sucks the most about your day?" works. "Would you use a tool that..." does not. Probe: "Could they answer that with yes or no? Rewrite it."

**Step 3 of 6: Find what sucks most**
> Of everything they might say sucks, how will you get them to rank it, and what do you think lands on top?

Push for: a ranking question ("If you could fix one thing tomorrow, what would it be?") and a written guess at the winner, tagged [Assumption] until they've heard it. Then the follow-up: what have they tried, and where did it let them down?

**Step 4 of 6: Picture what's awesome**
> Fill in the sentence for the top problem: "Instead of ___, wouldn't it be awesome if ___?"

Push for: the awesome half described as an outcome the customer feels, in words they'd use. If it names a feature, ask: "What would they be able to do, or stop doing, if that existed?"

**Step 5 of 6: Build exactly that**
> If that awesome thing is true, what has the product got to be, and what must it never be?

Push for: two or three "should be" words and two or three "should not be" words, plus one success criterion they could check. These seed #14 Experience Canvas.

**Step 6 of 6: Sell it in their words**
> How will you say their problem back to them so they say "exactly"?

Push for: a sell-back sentence built from their language: "So it sounds like what really hurts is ___." If the words are the founder's, ask: "Which of these would they actually say to a friend?"

## Push back when

- **The opener is about the product.** "You're pitching. This conversation is about their world. Ask what sucks about it."
- **They want to skip to awesome.** "You haven't found what sucks most yet. Rank the pain first, or you'll build for a small one."
- **The awesome column is a feature list.** "That's your solution. What's the result they'd feel?"
- **The words are polished.** "That's ad-speak. Their exact words are the copy. Go get them."
- **Every step is a guess and they want to keep planning.** "This is a plan, and a plan isn't evidence. Book the conversation."

**Coaching notes**: 3 to 5 sentences on what the plan revealed: how much came from real conversations, whether the top problem is the customer's or the founder's, and whether the language sounds like a customer.

**Assumptions to test**: each [Assumption] step with the interview question that would settle it.

**Your next move this week**: book one DSBA conversation with the person named in Step 1, and prepare the script with #3 DSBA Interview Guide.

## Done well

- The founder can name all six steps and what each one produces.
- The top problem and the awesome sentence came from a customer, or are clearly marked as guesses to test.
- The sell-back line uses words a customer actually said.
